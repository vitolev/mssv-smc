\chapter{Sequential Monte Carlo Methods}
\label{chap:sequential_monte_carlo}

Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) methods provide a flexible framework for approximating probability distributions that evolve sequentially over time. 
They are particularly useful for inference in state-space models, where the latent states are typically not directly observed and their posterior distributions are analytically intractable. 
SMC methods approximate these distributions using a set of weighted samples, commonly referred to as particles, and update this approximation as new observations become available.

This chapter first introduces the foundations of SMC, beginning with Monte Carlo methods, importance sampling and sequential importance sampling. 
The problem of weight degeneracy is then addressed through resampling, leading to sequential importance sampling with resampling, also known as particle filtering. 
The bootstrap particle filter is presented in more detail.

Next, the chapter considers particle-based methods for Bayesian parameter inference. 
Particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings and particle Gibbs are introduced as particle Markov chain Monte Carlo methods. 
Finally, SMC$^2$ is presented as an extension that combines particle filtering with sequential inference for static parameters and latent states.

\section{Monte Carlo methods}
Monte Carlo methods approximate quantities involving a probability distribution by using a finite collection of random samples. The basic idea is simple: rather than evaluating an integral directly, we replace it by an average over samples drawn from the distribution of interest.

Until further notice, let $X$ be a random variable with probability density function $p(x)$, and let $g$ be a measurable function such that $E_p[|g(X)|] < \infty$. The quantity of interest is
\begin{equation}
    I = E_p[g(X)] = \int g(x)p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.
    \label{eq:target_expectation}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}
    Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} p(x)$. The \emph{Monte Carlo estimator} of $I$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i).
        \label{eq:monte_carlo_estimator}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Such an estimator has several desirable properties, including unbiasedness, consistency, and asymptotic normality.

\begin{proposition}
    The Monte Carlo estimator $\hat{I}_N$ is unbiased.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    The proof follows from the linearity of expectation and the fact that the samples are identically distributed.
    \begin{align}
        E_p[\hat{I}_N] &= E_p\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i)\right] \\
        &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N E_p[g(X_i)] \\
        &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N I = I.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    The Monte Carlo estimator $\hat{I}_N$ is consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Because the samples are i.i.d.\ and $E_p[|g(X)|] < \infty$, the strong law of large numbers applies, giving
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} I.
    \end{equation}
    Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, so the estimator is consistent.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    If $g(X)$ has finite variance, then the Monte Carlo estimator $\hat{I}_N$ is asymptotically normal.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    If $g(X)$ has finite variance $\sigma^2$, then the central limit theorem applies, giving
    \begin{equation}
        \sqrt{N}\left(\hat{I}_N-I\right)
        \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{D}
        \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
    \end{equation}
    which is the definition of asymptotic normality.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Empirical distribution}
\label{sec:empirical_distribution}

The Monte Carlo estimator can be interpreted as integration with respect to an empirical approximation constructed from the samples.

\begin{definition}
Given samples $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} p(x)$, the \emph{empirical distribution} associated with these samples is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_N(x)
    = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x-X_i),
    \label{eq:empirical_distribution}
\end{equation}
where $\delta(\cdot)$ denotes the Dirac delta function.
\end{definition}

The empirical distribution assigns equal weight $1/N$ to each sampled value. It therefore provides a discrete approximation to the distribution represented by $p(x)$. The expression in~\eqref{eq:empirical_distribution} should be understood through integration rather than as an ordinary probability density with respect to Lebesgue measure.

\begin{proposition}
For $g$, $\hat{I}_N$ and $\hat{p}_N$ as previously defined,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{I}_N = \int g(x)\hat{p}_N(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    \begin{align}
        \int g(x)\hat{p}_N(x)\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int g(x) \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
        &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \int g(x)\delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
        &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N g(X_i) = \hat{I}_N,
    \end{align}
    where the defining property of the Dirac delta function has been used.
\end{proof}

It is important to distinguish the empirical distribution $\hat{p}_N$ from the density $p$. Even when $p$ is continuous, $\hat{p}_N$ consists of point masses for every finite $N$ and is therefore not a pointwise approximation of $p(x)$. The approximation is instead meaningful through quantities such as integrals and expectations.

\section{Importance sampling}
\label{sec:importance_sampling}
The standard Monte Carlo method requires samples from the target distribution $p$. In many problems, however, sampling directly from $p$ is difficult or even impossible.
Importance sampling addresses this problem by introducing a second distribution from which sampling is easier.

\begin{definition}
Let $q(x)$ be a proposal distribution satisfying $q(x)>0$ whenever $p(x)>0$. 
The \emph{importance weight} associated with a point $x$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.
    \label{eq:importance_weight}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The expectation of $g$ under $p$ can then be rewritten as
\begin{align}
    I = E_p[g(X)] &= \int g(x)p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &= \int g(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &= E_q\left[g(X)w(X)\right].
\end{align}
Thus, although samples are generated from $q$, the importance weights correct for the difference between $q$ and $p$.

\begin{definition}
Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ and $w(X_1),\ldots,w(X_N)$ their corresponding importance weights.
The \emph{importance sampling estimator} of $I=E_p[g(X)]$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N g(X_i)w(X_i).
    \label{eq:importance_sampling_estimator}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:importance_sampling_unbiased}
    The importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}$ is unbiased.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    \begin{align}
        E_q[\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}] &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N E_q[g(X_i)w(X_i)] \\
        &= E_q\left[g(X)\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\right] \\
        &= \int g(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = I.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:importance_sampling_consistent}
    The importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}$ is consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Because $E_q[|g(X)w(X)|] = E_p[|g(X)|] < \infty$, the strong law of large numbers gives
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}} \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} I,
    \end{equation}
    which means that the estimator is consistent.
\end{proof}

Although the importance sampling estimator is unbiased and consistent for any proposal distribution satisfying the support condition, its variance depends strongly on the choice of $q$.

\subsection{(Weighted) empirical distribution}
\label{sec:weighted_empirical_distribution}

There are two equivalent ways to interpret the importance sampling estimator.
The first one follows the same logic as in Section~\ref{sec:empirical_distribution}:
\begin{align}
    \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w(X_i)g(X_i) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \int w(x)g(x) \delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &=\int w(x)g(x) \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &=\int w(x)g(x) \hat{q}_N(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,
\end{align}
where $\hat{q}_N$ is the empirical distribution of the samples $X_1,\ldots,X_N\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}q$, so the estimator is simply the expectation of $w(X)g(X)$ with respect to $\hat{q}_N$.

There is another interpretation that is particularly useful. Instead of viewing the weights as part of the integrand, we can absorb them into the empirical distribution itself.
\begin{align}
    \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w(X_i)g(X_i) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w(X_i) \int g(x) \delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &=\int g(x) \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w(X_i)\delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &=\int g(x) \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,
\end{align}
leading us to next definition.
\begin{definition}
Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ and $w(X_1),\ldots,w(X_N)$ their corresponding importance weights. 
The \emph{weighted empirical distribution} is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}(x) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N w(X_i)\delta(x-X_i).
    \label{eq:weighted_empirical_distribution}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

This provides an alternative interpretation of importance sampling:
although the samples are drawn from the proposal distribution $q$, the
importance weights allow the samples to represent the target distribution
$p$. Unlike ordinary Monte Carlo, where every sample contributes the same
mass $1/N$, importance sampling assigns the sample $X_i$ a mass
proportional to $w(X_i)$.

The weighted empirical distribution in
\eqref{eq:weighted_empirical_distribution}, however, is not generally a
probability distribution for a finite sample. Its total mass is
\begin{equation}
    \int \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N w(X_i),
\end{equation}
which is a random quantity and is not necessarily equal to one.
Nevertheless, the total mass has expectation equal to one. Since $X\sim q$,
\begin{align}
    E_q[w(X)] &= \int \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1, \\
    E_q\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N w(X_i)\right] &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N E_q[w(X_i)] = 1.
\end{align}
Additionally, the strong law of large numbers gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N w(X_i) \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} E_q[w(X)] = 1.
\end{equation}
Thus, although the weighted empirical distribution does not necessarily
have unit mass for a finite sample, its total mass converges almost surely
to one as the number of samples increases.

\section{Self-normalized importance sampling}
\label{sec:self_normalized_importance_sampling}
The previous construction suggests a natural modification: instead of allowing the weights to have a random total mass, normalize them. Writing $w_i = w(X_i)$ for brevity, the \emph{normalized importance weights} are
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{w}_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} w_j},
\end{equation}
which leads to the following definition.
\begin{definition}
    Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ and $\tilde{w}_1,\ldots,\tilde{w}_N$ their corresponding normalized importance weights.
    The \emph{self-normalized weighted empirical distribution} is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_i \delta(x-X_i).
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
This distribution clearly has a total mass of one.
\begin{definition}
    Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ and $w_1,\ldots,w_N$ their corresponding importance weights.
    The \emph{self-normalized importance sampling estimator} of $I=E_p[g(X)]$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i)w_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    For $g$, $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ and $\hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ as previously defined,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}} = \int g(x) \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}(x)\, \mathrm{d}x.
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    \begin{align}
        \int g(x) \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}(x)\, \mathrm{d}x &= \int g(x) \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_i \delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
        &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_i \int g(x) \delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
        &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_i g(X_i) \\
        &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i)w_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i}.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

There is a fundamental difference between $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}$ and $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$. The ordinary importance sampling estimator is unbiased, whereas the self-normalized estimator is generally biased for finite $N$.
\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_biased}
    The self-normalized importance sampling estimator is, in general, biased.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    In general, the expected value of a ratio is not equal to the ratio of expected values, so
    \begin{align}
        E_q\left[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(X_i)w_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i}\right] &\neq \frac{E_q\left[\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}g(X_i)w_i\right]}{E_q\left[\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\right]}, \\
        E_q\left[\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}\right] &\neq \frac{E_q\left[\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}\right]}{1} = I.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_consistent}
    The self-normalized importance sampling estimator is consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    The strong law of large numbers gives
    \begin{align}
        \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i)w_i &\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} I,\\
        \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i &\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} 1.
    \end{align}
    Consequently,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}} = \frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i)w_i}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i} \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} I.
    \end{equation}
\end{proof}

At first, it may seem pointless to use the self-normalized estimator merely to ensure that $\hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ is a proper probability distribution, since the estimator loses its unbiasedness. 
However, in many applications, especially in Bayesian inference, the target density $p(x)$ is known only up to a proportionality constant. Suppose that the target has the form
\begin{equation}
    p(x) = \frac{\tilde{p}(x)}{Z},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{p}(x)$ is known and $Z$ is an intractable normalization constant. The importance weights are then
\begin{equation}
    w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \frac{\tilde{p}(x)}{Zq(x)},
\end{equation}
which makes it impossible to compute the ordinary importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}$. However, the normalized weights are still computable:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{w}_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j=1}^N w_j} = \frac{\frac{\tilde{p}(X_i)}{Zq(X_i)}}{\sum_{j=1}^N \frac{\tilde{p}(X_j)}{Zq(X_j)}} = \frac{\frac{\tilde{p}(X_i)}{q(X_i)}}{\sum_{j=1}^N \frac{\tilde{p}(X_j)}{q(X_j)}}.
\end{equation}
Therefore, the self-normalized importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ can be used even when the target density is known only up to a constant of proportionality, at the cost of introducing bias.

\section{Sequential importance sampling}
Sequential importance sampling (SIS) applies importance sampling to a sequence of random variables by exploiting the sequential structure of the problem.
Because the main focus of this thesis is on state-space models, in particular the MSSV model, we consider sequential importance sampling in the context of state-space models.

For the moment, assume that the model parameters $\theta$ are known. Given the observations $y_{1:T}$, the objective is to approximate the posterior distribution of the latent states, $p(x_{0:T} \mid y_{1:T},\theta)$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:joint_density}, the joint density is
\begin{equation}
p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \chi(x_0\mid\theta) \prod_{t=1}^T f(y_t\mid x_t,\theta) p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta).
\end{equation}
For the MSSV model, the latent state $x_t = (h_t,s_t)$ has a continuous component $h_t$ and a discrete component $s_t \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$. All densities of the latent states are therefore understood with respect to the product of the Lebesgue measure and the counting measure, so that integrals over $x_t$ are shorthand for
\begin{equation}
    \int g(x_t)\,\mathrm{d}x_t = \sum_{s_t=1}^{K} \int g(h_t,s_t)\,\mathrm{d}h_t.
\end{equation}
Correspondingly, $\delta(x_t - X_t)$ acts as a Dirac delta in $h_t$ and as a Kronecker delta in $s_t$. With this convention, all results of this chapter apply to the MSSV model without change.

Until further notice, assume that the proposal distribution can also be factorized sequentially as
\begin{equation}
q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta) = q_0(x_0\mid\theta) \prod_{t=1}^T q_t(x_t\mid x_{0:t-1},y_{1:t},\theta),
\end{equation}
from which we can sample entire trajectories of the latent states, conditioned on the currently available observations, in a sequential manner.

The importance weight can therefore be computed recursively as
\begin{align}
    w_T &= \frac{p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}{q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)} \\
    &= \frac{\chi(x_0\mid\theta) \prod_{t=1}^T f(y_t\mid x_t,\theta) p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta)}{q_0(x_0\mid\theta) \prod_{t=1}^T q_t(x_t\mid x_{0:t-1},y_{1:t},\theta)} \\
    &= w_{T-1} \cdot \frac{f(y_T\mid x_T,\theta) p(x_T\mid x_{T-1},\theta)}{q_T(x_T\mid x_{0:T-1},y_{1:T},\theta)} \\
    &= w_{T-1} \alpha_T = \prod_{t=0}^{T} \alpha_t
\end{align}
with $\alpha_0 = w_0 = \frac{\chi(x_0\mid\theta)}{q_0(x_0\mid\theta)}$.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:marginal_likelihood}
    The \emph{marginal likelihood} is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \int p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
Introducing the importance weights, the marginal likelihood can therefore be written as
\begin{equation}
    p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \int w_T\, q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} = E_q[w_T].
\end{equation}
For nonlinear and non-Gaussian state-space models, the marginal likelihood is typically intractable and therefore has to be estimated.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:marginal_likelihood_estimator}
    Let $X_{0:T}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{0:T}^{(N)} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ be sampled trajectories and $w_T^{(i)}$ their corresponding importance weights.
    Then the \emph{marginal likelihood estimator} is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_T^{(i)}.
        \label{eq:marginal_likelihood_estimator}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

As a result the marginal likelihood estimator can therefore be written as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \prod_{t=0}^{T} \alpha_t^{(i)}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:marginal_likelihood_estimator_unbiased_consistent}
    The marginal likelihood estimator $\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is unbiased and consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Unbiasedness follows from
    \begin{align}
        E_q[\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)] &= E_q\left[\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_T^{(i)}\right] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} E_q[w_T^{(i)}] \\
        &= E_q[w_T] = \int w_T q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} \\
        &= \int p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta),
    \end{align}
    where the linearity of expectation and the fact that the trajectories are identically distributed have been used.
    Since the weights $w_T^{(i)}$ are i.i.d., non-negative and have finite expectation $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$, consistency follows from the strong law of large numbers,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta).
    \end{equation}
    Alternatively, $\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is the (ordinary) importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}$ with $g\equiv 1$, hence the proofs of Propositions~\ref{prop:importance_sampling_unbiased} and~\ref{prop:importance_sampling_consistent} carry over directly.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    The \emph{smoothing distribution} is the posterior distribution of the entire latent trajectory given all available observations,
    \begin{equation}
        p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta) = \frac{p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}.
    \end{equation}
    The \emph{smoothing expectation} of a function $g$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        I^{\mathrm{smth}} = E\left[g(X_{0:T}) \mid y_{1:T},\theta\right] = \int g(x_{0:T}) p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Rewriting the smoothing expectation in terms of the proposal distribution gives
\begin{align}
    I^{\mathrm{smth}} &= \frac{1}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)} \int g(x_{0:T}) \frac{p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}{q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)} q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} \\
    &= \frac{E_q\left[g(X_{0:T})w_T\right]}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}
    = \frac{E_q\left[g(X_{0:T})w_T\right]}{E_q\left[w_T\right]}.
\end{align}
The smoothing distribution is thus a target known only up to the normalizing constant $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$, with unnormalized density $p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$, which is exactly the setting of Section~\ref{sec:self_normalized_importance_sampling}. Replacing both expectations by Monte Carlo averages over the sampled trajectories motivates the following definitions.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:smoothing_estimator}
    Let $X_{0:T}^{(i)} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ be sampled trajectories, $w_T^{(i)}$ their importance weights, and
    \begin{equation}
        \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} = \frac{w_T^{(i)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} w_T^{(j)}}
    \end{equation}
    the corresponding normalized weights. The \emph{smoothing estimator} of $I^{\mathrm{smth}}$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_{0:T}^{(i)}) w_T^{(i)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_T^{(i)}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} g(X_{0:T}^{(i)}),
        \label{eq:smoothing_estimator}
    \end{equation}
    inducing the \emph{empirical smoothing distribution} as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \delta(x_{0:T}-X_{0:T}^{(i)}).
        \label{eq:empirical_smoothing_distribution}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:smoothing_estimator}
    For $g$, $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$ and $\hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$ as previously defined,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}} = \int g(x_{0:T}) \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}.
    \end{equation}
    Moreover, the smoothing estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$ is, in general, biased, but consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    The first claim follows from the defining property of the Dirac delta function,
    \begin{align}
        \int g(x_{0:T}) \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \int g(x_{0:T}) \delta(x_{0:T}-X_{0:T}^{(i)})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} \\
        &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} g(X_{0:T}^{(i)}) = \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}.
    \end{align}
    The estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$ is the self-normalized importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ with target $p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)$, unnormalized target $p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$, normalizing constant $Z = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ and proposal $q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)$. 
    Bias and consistency therefore follow from Propositions~\ref{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_biased} and~\ref{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_consistent}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    The \emph{filtering distribution} is the marginal of the smoothing distribution,
    \begin{equation}
        p(x_T \mid y_{1:T},\theta) = \int p(x_{0:T} \mid y_{1:T},\theta) \,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T-1}.
    \end{equation}
    The \emph{filtering expectation} of a function $g$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        I^{\mathrm{filt}} = E\left[g(X_T) \mid y_{1:T},\theta\right] = \int g(x_T)p(x_T \mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_T.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

In the empirical setting, the states at previous times are easily integrated out, since $\delta(x_{0:T}-X_{0:T}^{(i)}) = \delta(x_T-X_T^{(i)})\,\delta(x_{0:T-1}-X_{0:T-1}^{(i)})$,
\begin{align}
    \int \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}(x_{0:T} &\mid y_{1:T}, \theta) \,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T-1} \\
    &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \delta(x_T-X_T^{(i)}) \int \delta(x_{0:T-1}-X_{0:T-1}^{(i)}) \,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T-1} \\
    &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \delta(x_T-X_T^{(i)}),
\end{align}
which leads to the following definition.

\begin{definition}
    Let $X_{0:T}^{(i)} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ and $\tilde{w}_T^{(i)}$ their corresponding normalized weights. 
    The \emph{filtering estimator} of $I^{\mathrm{filt}}$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_{T}^{(i)} g(X_T^{(i)}),
    \end{equation}
    inducing the \emph{empirical filtering distribution} as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}(x_T \mid y_{1:T},\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \delta(x_T-X_T^{(i)}).
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:filtering_estimator}
    The filtering estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}$ is, in general, biased, but consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Define the function $\bar{g}(x_{0:T}) = g(x_T)$, which depends on the trajectory only through its final state. Its smoothing expectation is
    \begin{align}
        E\left[\bar{g}(X_{0:T}) \mid y_{1:T},\theta\right] &= \int g(x_T)\, p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} \\
        &= \int g(x_T) \left(\int p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T-1}\right)\mathrm{d}x_T \\
        &= \int g(x_T)\, p(x_T\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_T = I^{\mathrm{filt}},
    \end{align}
    where the previous states have been integrated out. Likewise, the smoothing estimator of this expectation is
    \begin{equation}
        \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \bar{g}(X_{0:T}^{(i)}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} g(X_T^{(i)}) = \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}.
    \end{equation}
    Thus, $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}$ is the smoothing estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$ applied to $\bar{g}$, and its bias and consistency follow from Proposition~\ref{prop:smoothing_estimator}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    The (one-step) \emph{predictive distribution} is obtained by propagating the filtering distribution through the transition density,
    \begin{equation}
        p(x_{T+1} \mid y_{1:T}, \theta) = \int p(x_{T+1} \mid x_T, \theta)p(x_T \mid y_{1:T}, \theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_T.
    \end{equation}
    The \emph{predictive expectation} of a function $g$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        I^{\mathrm{pred}} = E\left[g(X_{T+1}) \mid y_{1:T}, \theta\right] = \int g(x_{T+1})p(x_{T+1} \mid y_{1:T}, \theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{T+1}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Replacing the filtering distribution with its empirical approximation gives
\begin{equation}
    \int p(x_{T+1} \mid x_T, \theta) \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}(x_T \mid y_{1:T}, \theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_T = \sum_{i=1}^N \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} p(x_{T+1} \mid X_T^{(i)}, \theta),
\end{equation}
a mixture of transition densities weighted by the normalized weights. A particle approximation of this mixture is obtained by propagating each particle through its transition density, which leads to the following definition.

\begin{definition}
    Let $X_{0:T}^{(i)}$ and $\tilde{w}_T^{(i)}$ be as in Definition~\ref{def:smoothing_estimator}, and let $X_{T+1}^{(i)} \sim p(x_{T+1} \mid X_T^{(i)}, \theta)$ be sampled independently. 
    The \emph{predictive estimator} of $I^{\mathrm{pred}}$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_{T}^{(i)} g(X_{T+1}^{(i)}),
        \label{eq:predictive_estimator}
    \end{equation}
    inducing the \emph{empirical predictive distribution} as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{pred}}(x_{T+1} \mid y_{1:T}, \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \delta(x_{T+1}-X_{T+1}^{(i)}).
        \label{eq:empirical_predictive_distribution}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:predictive_estimator}
    The predictive estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}}$ is, in general, biased, but consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    The extended trajectories $X_{0:T+1}^{(i)}$ are independent draws from the extended proposal
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{q}(x_{0:T+1}\mid y_{1:T},\theta) = q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,p(x_{T+1}\mid x_T,\theta).
    \end{equation}
    Take as the target the extended smoothing distribution
    \begin{equation}
        p(x_{0:T+1}\mid y_{1:T},\theta) = \frac{p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(x_{T+1}\mid x_T,\theta)}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)},
    \end{equation}
    whose marginal in $x_{T+1}$ is the predictive distribution, so that
    \begin{equation}
        I^{\mathrm{pred}} = \int g(x_{T+1})\,p(x_{0:T+1}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T+1}.
    \end{equation}
    The transition densities cancel in the corresponding unnormalized importance weight,
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{w}_{T+1} = \frac{p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(x_{T+1}\mid x_T,\theta)}{q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,p(x_{T+1}\mid x_T,\theta)} = w_T.
    \end{equation}
    Rewriting the predictive expectation in terms of the extended proposal therefore gives
    \begin{align}
        I^{\mathrm{pred}} &= \frac{1}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)} \int g(x_{T+1})\, \bar{w}_{T+1}\, \bar{q}(x_{0:T+1}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T+1} \\
        &= \frac{E_{\bar{q}}\left[g(X_{T+1})\,w_T\right]}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}.
    \end{align}
    Analogously, the normalizing constant is
    \begin{equation}
        p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \int \bar{w}_{T+1}\, \bar{q}(x_{0:T+1}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T+1} = E_{\bar{q}}\left[w_T\right],
    \end{equation}
    which yields
    \begin{equation}
        I^{\mathrm{pred}} = \frac{E_{\bar{q}}\left[g(X_{T+1})\,w_T\right]}{E_{\bar{q}}\left[w_T\right]}.
    \end{equation}
    Replacing both expectations by Monte Carlo averages over $X_{0:T+1}^{(i)} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \bar{q}$ gives
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_{T+1}^{(i)})\, w_T^{(i)}}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_T^{(i)}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} g(X_{T+1}^{(i)}) = \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}}.
    \end{equation}
    Hence $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}}$ is exactly the self-normalized importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ of $I^{\mathrm{pred}}$ with proposal $\bar{q}$, unnormalized target $p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(x_{T+1}\mid x_T,\theta)$ and normalizing constant $Z = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$.
    Bias and consistency follow from Propositions~\ref{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_biased} and~\ref{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_consistent}.
\end{proof}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Sequential importance sampling}
\label{alg:sequential_importance_sampling}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Observations $y_{1:T}$, number of particles $N$, proposal densities $q_0,q_t$, and model densities $\chi,p,f$.

    \FOR{$i=1,\ldots,N$}
        \STATE Sample $X_0^{(i)} \sim q_0(x_0 \mid \theta)$.
        \STATE Set $w_0^{(i)} = \dfrac{\chi(X_0^{(i)}\mid \theta)}{q_0(X_0^{(i)} \mid \theta)}$.
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Normalize weights to obtain $\tilde{w}_0^{(i)}$.

    \FOR{$t=1,\ldots,T$}
        \FOR{$i=1,\ldots,N$}
            \STATE Propagate $X_t^{(i)} \sim q_t(\cdot\mid X_{0:t-1}^{(i)},y_{1:t},\theta)$.
            \STATE Compute $\alpha_t^{(i)} = \dfrac{f(y_t\mid X_t^{(i)}, \theta)p(X_t^{(i)}\mid X_{t-1}^{(i)}, \theta)}{q_t(X_t^{(i)}\mid X_{0:t-1}^{(i)},y_{1:t}, \theta)}$.
            \STATE Update $w_t^{(i)} = w_{t-1}^{(i)}\alpha_t^{(i)}$.
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE Normalize weights to obtain $\tilde{w}_t^{(i)}$.
    \ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
In summary, the sequential updates of the importance weights together with the propagation of particles allow us to approximate the smoothing, filtering, and predictive distributions, and their expectations, in a sequential manner. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:sequential_importance_sampling}.
Since all of $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$, $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}$ and $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}}$ are self-normalized, they are biased but consistent estimators. Only the marginal likelihood estimator $\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is unbiased.

The main limitation of SIS is \emph{weight degeneracy}. As the number of observations increases, the importance weights become increasingly unequal, because only a small fraction of the particle trajectories are able to explain all of the observations. Eventually, almost all of the normalized weight is concentrated on a few particles, and the remaining particles contribute virtually nothing to the estimates. This motivates the idea of resampling.

\section{Sequential importance sampling with resampling}
\label{sec:sequential_importance_sampling_with_resampling}
Sequential importance sampling with resampling (SISR), also known as particle filtering, mitigates weight degeneracy by adding a resampling step to SIS. After the weights are updated, particles with low weights are discarded and particles with high weights are replicated, so that the computational effort is concentrated on regions of high posterior probability. Each iteration thus consists of three steps: resampling, propagation and weighting.

\subsection{Resampling}
\label{sec:resampling}
Consider particles $X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(N)}$ with normalized weights $\tilde{w}^{(1)},\ldots,\tilde{w}^{(N)}$, where the time index is omitted for the moment.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:resampling}
    A \emph{resampling scheme} draws random \emph{ancestor indices}
    \begin{equation}
        A^{(1)},\ldots,A^{(N)} \in \{1,\ldots,N\}
    \end{equation}
    according to the normalized weights $\tilde{w}^{(1:N)}$. The $i$-th particle after resampling is a copy of the particle $X^{(A^{(i)})}$, its \emph{ancestor}, and all resampled particles receive the weight $1/N$. The number of \emph{offspring} of particle $j$ is
    \begin{equation}
        N^{(j)} = \left|\left\{i : A^{(i)} = j\right\}\right|, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{N} N^{(j)} = N.
    \end{equation}
    A resampling scheme is \emph{unbiased} if
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[N^{(j)}\mid \tilde{w}^{(1:N)}\right] = N\tilde{w}^{(j)}, \qquad j=1,\ldots,N,
        \label{eq:unbiased_resampling}
    \end{equation}
    where the expectation is taken with respect to the randomness introduced by resampling.
\end{definition}

The unbiasedness condition states that, on average, each particle is copied a number of times proportional to its weight. The most common resampling schemes are multinomial, stratified, systematic and residual resampling, which are all unbiased. 
Systematic resampling is used in this thesis. It requires only one uniform random variable and $O(N)$ operations, and it typically introduces less additional variance than multinomial resampling.

Resampling replaces the weighted particles by equally weighted ones. In terms of empirical distributions, the self-normalized weighted empirical distribution is replaced by the \emph{resampled empirical distribution},
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}^{(i)}\delta(x-X^{(i)}) \quad \longrightarrow \quad \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x-X^{(A^{(i)})}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}N^{(j)}\delta(x-X^{(j)}).
    \label{eq:resampled_empirical_distribution}
\end{equation}
For a function $g$, the estimate before resampling and the corresponding estimate after resampling are therefore
\begin{align}
    \hat{I}_N &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}^{(i)} g(X^{(i)}), \\
    \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{res}} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X^{(A^{(i)})}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} N^{(j)}g(X^{(j)}).
\end{align}
The two generally differ for a particular resampling outcome, but they agree on average.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:resampling_unbiased}
    For an unbiased resampling scheme, the resampled estimator is conditionally unbiased,
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{res}} \mid X^{(1:N)}, \tilde{w}^{(1:N)}\right] = \hat{I}_N.
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Given the particles and the weights, the values $g(X^{(j)})$ are fixed, and the only randomness comes from the offspring counts, which depend only on the weights. By the linearity of expectation and~\eqref{eq:unbiased_resampling},
    \begin{align}
        E\left[\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{res}} \mid X^{(1:N)}, \tilde{w}^{(1:N)}\right] &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} g(X^{(j)})\, E\left[N^{(j)} \mid \tilde{w}^{(1:N)}\right] \\
        &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} g(X^{(j)})\, N\tilde{w}^{(j)} = \hat{I}_N.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

Resampling thus leaves the estimates unchanged in expectation, but it adds Monte Carlo variability. By the law of total variance,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{res}}\right) = \mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{I}_N\right) + E\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{res}} \mid X^{(1:N)}, \tilde{w}^{(1:N)}\right)\right] \ge \mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{I}_N\right).
\end{equation}
Estimates at the current time step should therefore be computed from the weighted particles, before resampling. The benefit of resampling lies in the future: particles with negligible weights are no longer propagated, and the particles at later time steps are more diverse. Resampling is thus beneficial only when the weights are sufficiently unequal. A common measure of weight inequality is the following.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:ess}
    The \emph{effective sample size} of the normalized weights $\tilde{w}^{(1:N)}$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{ESS} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(\tilde{w}^{(i)}\right)^2}.
        \label{eq:ess}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The effective sample size can be interpreted as the number of equally weighted particles that would give a comparable approximation. Resampling can be performed adaptively, only when the effective sample size falls below a chosen threshold. In this thesis, however, we resample at every time step, which is what we assume from now on.

\subsection{Particle filter}
\label{sec:particle_filter}
We now return to the sequential setting and add the time index, so that $A_t^{(i)}$ and $N_t^{(i)}$ denote the ancestor indices and the offspring counts of the resampling step that is applied to the particles at time $t$. SISR samples the initial particles exactly as SIS,
\begin{equation}
    X_0^{(i)} \sim q_0(x_0\mid\theta), \qquad \alpha_0^{(i)} = \frac{\chi(X_0^{(i)}\mid\theta)}{q_0(X_0^{(i)}\mid\theta)},
\end{equation}
and, for $t\ge 1$, it first resamples the particles at time $t-1$ and then propagates the resampled particles,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        X_t^{(i)} &\sim q_t\left(x_t\mid X_{0:t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})},y_{1:t},\theta\right), \\
        \alpha_t^{(i)} &= \frac{f(y_t\mid X_t^{(i)},\theta)\,p\left(X_t^{(i)}\mid X_{t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})},\theta\right)}{q_t\left(X_t^{(i)}\mid X_{0:t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})},y_{1:t},\theta\right)},
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:sisr_propagation}
\end{equation}
independently for $i=1,\ldots,N$. Here $X_{0:t}^{(i)}$ denotes the trajectory ending in $X_t^{(i)}$, defined recursively by
\begin{equation}
    X_{0:t}^{(i)} = \left(X_{0:t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})},\, X_t^{(i)}\right).
    \label{eq:sisr_trajectory}
\end{equation}
In practice, the trajectories are not copied at every resampling step. Instead, the ancestor indices are stored, and the trajectory of any particle is reconstructed by following its ancestor indices backwards in time.

In SIS, the weight of a particle is the product of all its incremental weights, $w_t^{(i)} = w_{t-1}^{(i)}\alpha_t^{(i)}$. In SISR, all weights equal $1/N$ after resampling, so the normalized weights depend only on the latest incremental weights,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{w}_t^{(i)} = \frac{\frac{1}{N}\alpha_t^{(i)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\alpha_t^{(j)}} = \frac{\alpha_t^{(i)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_t^{(j)}}.
    \label{eq:sisr_normalized_weights}
\end{equation}
The complete procedure is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:sisr}. The quantities $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_t$ computed along the way are explained in Section~\ref{sec:pf_marginal_likelihood}.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Sequential importance sampling with resampling (particle filter)}
\label{alg:sisr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Observations $y_{1:T}$, number of particles $N$, parameters $\theta$, proposal densities $q_0,q_t$, model densities $\chi,p,f$, and an unbiased resampling scheme.

    \FOR{$i=1,\ldots,N$}
        \STATE Sample $X_0^{(i)} \sim q_0(x_0 \mid \theta)$.
        \STATE Compute $\alpha_0^{(i)} = \dfrac{\chi(X_0^{(i)}\mid \theta)}{q_0(X_0^{(i)} \mid \theta)}$.
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Set $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_0 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_0^{(j)}$.
    \STATE Normalize $\tilde{w}_0^{(i)} = \alpha_0^{(i)} / \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_0^{(j)}$ for $i=1,\ldots,N$.

    \FOR{$t=1,\ldots,T$}
        \STATE Resample: draw ancestor indices $A_{t-1}^{(1:N)}$ according to $\tilde{w}_{t-1}^{(1:N)}$.
        \FOR{$i=1,\ldots,N$}
            \STATE Propagate $X_t^{(i)} \sim q_t(\cdot\mid X_{0:t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})},y_{1:t},\theta)$ and set $X_{0:t}^{(i)} = \big(X_{0:t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})}, X_t^{(i)}\big)$.
            \STATE Compute $\alpha_t^{(i)} = \dfrac{f(y_t\mid X_t^{(i)}, \theta)\,p(X_t^{(i)}\mid X_{t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})}, \theta)}{q_t(X_t^{(i)}\mid X_{0:t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})},y_{1:t}, \theta)}$.
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE Update $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_t = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_t^{(j)}$.
        \STATE Normalize $\tilde{w}_t^{(i)} = \alpha_t^{(i)} / \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_t^{(j)}$ for $i=1,\ldots,N$.
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN Particles $X_{0:T}^{(1:N)}$, normalized weights $\tilde{w}_T^{(1:N)}$ and $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The smoothing, filtering and predictive distributions are approximated in the same way as in SIS, with the trajectories~\eqref{eq:sisr_trajectory} and the normalized weights~\eqref{eq:sisr_normalized_weights}. At every time $t$, the empirical smoothing and filtering distributions of the particle filter are
\begin{align}
    \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}(x_{0:t}\mid y_{1:t},\theta) &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_t^{(i)} \delta(x_{0:t}-X_{0:t}^{(i)}), \label{eq:pf_empirical_smoothing_distribution}\\
    \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}(x_t\mid y_{1:t},\theta) &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_t^{(i)} \delta(x_t-X_t^{(i)}), \label{eq:pf_empirical_filtering_distribution}
\end{align}
and the corresponding estimators are $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_t^{(i)}g(X_{0:t}^{(i)})$ and $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_t^{(i)}g(X_t^{(i)})$. For the predictive distribution, each particle is propagated through the transition density, $X_{t+1}^{(i)}\sim p(x_{t+1}\mid X_t^{(i)},\theta)$, which gives
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{pred}}(x_{t+1}\mid y_{1:t},\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_t^{(i)} \delta(x_{t+1}-X_{t+1}^{(i)})
    \label{eq:pf_empirical_predictive_distribution}
\end{equation}
and $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_t^{(i)}g(X_{t+1}^{(i)})$, exactly as in~\eqref{eq:predictive_estimator} and~\eqref{eq:empirical_predictive_distribution}.

There is, however, an important difference in the justification of these estimators. Resampling makes the particles dependent, since several particles may share the same ancestor. The trajectories are therefore no longer i.i.d.\ draws from a fixed proposal, and Propositions~\ref{prop:smoothing_estimator}, \ref{prop:filtering_estimator} and~\ref{prop:predictive_estimator}, which rely on the strong law of large numbers for i.i.d.\ samples, no longer apply. The estimators are still self-normalized and thus, in general, biased. They remain consistent as $N\to\infty$ under mild conditions, but the proof requires an induction over the time steps and is considerably more involved; see \cite[Chapter~11]{chopin2020smc}.

Figure~\ref{fig:resampling} illustrates the effect of resampling on the particle trajectories. A side effect of repeated resampling is \emph{path degeneracy}: going backwards in time, the trajectories coalesce to only a few common ancestors, and eventually to a single one. The empirical smoothing distribution~\eqref{eq:pf_empirical_smoothing_distribution} therefore has very few distinct values for the early states, and its approximation of $p(x_{0:t}\mid y_{1:t},\theta)$ deteriorates for those states, even though the approximation of the filtering distribution remains accurate. Resampling thus trades weight degeneracy, which affects all estimates, for path degeneracy, which affects only the estimates that involve states far in the past.

\subsection{Marginal likelihood estimation}
\label{sec:pf_marginal_likelihood}
Parameter inference in Section~\ref{sec:particle_mcmc} requires an unbiased estimate of the marginal likelihood $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$. The SIS estimator~\eqref{eq:marginal_likelihood_estimator}, the average of the unnormalized weights, is unbiased, but its variance typically grows exponentially in $T$ because of weight degeneracy. With resampling it is not even available, since resampling uses only the normalized weights and then resets all weights to $1/N$, so the scale of the unnormalized weights is lost.

The remedy is to keep track of the lost scale. If each resampled particle were assigned the average weight before resampling instead of $1/N$, the average weight would be preserved by resampling and multiplied by $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{t}^{(i)}$ at each time step. This motivates the following definition.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:pf_marginal_likelihood_estimator}
    Let $\alpha_t^{(i)}$ be the incremental weights of Algorithm~\ref{alg:sisr}. The \emph{particle filter marginal likelihood estimator} is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_t = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:t}\mid\theta) = \prod_{s=0}^{t} \left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_s^{(i)}\right), \qquad t=0,\ldots,T.
        \label{eq:pf_marginal_likelihood_estimator}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
The SIS estimator is an average over particles of products of incremental weights, whereas $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_t$ is a product over time of averages. The two coincide for $N=1$. For $s\ge 1$, each factor can be interpreted as an estimate of the conditional density $p(y_s\mid y_{1:s-1},\theta)$, in line with the decomposition
\begin{equation}
    p(y_{1:t}\mid\theta) = \prod_{s=1}^{t} p(y_s\mid y_{1:s-1},\theta).
\end{equation}

Unbiasedness of $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_t$ does not follow directly from the results so far. Proposition~\ref{prop:marginal_likelihood_estimator_unbiased_consistent} requires i.i.d.\ trajectories, which resampling destroys. Nor are the individual factors unbiased, since the particles only approximate the predictive distribution, and even if they were, the factors are dependent, so the expectation of their product is not the product of their expectations. The following lemma instead chains the resampling and propagation steps. Multiplying by $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}$ cancels the normalization of the weights, which is what makes the argument work.

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:pf_unnormalized_unbiased}
    Assume that the proposals satisfy the support condition of importance sampling, that the incremental weights are strictly positive, and that the resampling scheme is unbiased and, given the normalized weights, independent of everything generated before the resampling step. Then for every $t=0,\ldots,T$ and every non-negative function $\varphi$ of trajectories $x_{0:t}$,
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1} \, \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_t^{(i)}\varphi(X_{0:t}^{(i)})\right] = \int \varphi(x_{0:t})\,p(x_{0:t},y_{1:t}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:t},
        \label{eq:pf_unnormalized_unbiased}
    \end{equation}
    with the conventions $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{-1} = 1$ and $p(x_{0:0},y_{1:0}\mid\theta) = \chi(x_0\mid\theta)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    The proof is by induction on $t$.

    \emph{Base case.} For $t=0$, the trajectories $X_{0:0}^{(i)} = X_0^{(i)}$ are i.i.d.\ draws from $q_0$ with importance weights $\alpha_0^{(i)}$, so the left-hand side of~\eqref{eq:pf_unnormalized_unbiased} is the expectation of an ordinary importance sampling estimator with unnormalized target $\chi$. As in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:importance_sampling_unbiased},
    \begin{align}
        E\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_0^{(i)}\varphi(X_0^{(i)})\right] &= \int \varphi(x_0)\frac{\chi(x_0\mid\theta)}{q_0(x_0\mid\theta)}q_0(x_0\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_0 \\
        &= \int \varphi(x_0)\,\chi(x_0\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_0.
    \end{align}

    \emph{Induction step.} Let $t\ge 1$ and assume that the claim holds at time $t-1$ for every non-negative function. Let $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ denote all random variables generated up to and including the resampling of the particles at time $t-1$, that is, the particles $X_{0:t-1}^{(1:N)}$ and the ancestor indices $A_{0:t-1}^{(1:N)}$, and let $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{-}$ denote those generated before this resampling step, that is, $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ without $A_{t-1}^{(1:N)}$.

    \emph{Propagation.} Given $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$, the particle $X_t^{(i)}$ is drawn from the proposal in~\eqref{eq:sisr_propagation}, which cancels against the denominator of $\alpha_t^{(i)}$. By~\eqref{eq:sisr_trajectory}, $X_{0:t}^{(i)} = (X_{0:t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})},X_t^{(i)})$, so
    \begin{align}
        E\left[\alpha_t^{(i)}\,\varphi(X_{0:t}^{(i)})\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right] &= \int f(y_t\mid x_t,\theta)\,\varphi\left(X_{0:t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})},x_t\right) \\
        &\qquad\quad \times p\left(x_t\mid X_{t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})},\theta\right)\mathrm{d}x_t \\
        &= \bar{\varphi}\left(X_{0:t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})}\right),
    \end{align}
    where
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{\varphi}(x_{0:t-1}) = \int f(y_t\mid x_t,\theta)\,\varphi(x_{0:t-1},x_t)\,p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_t
    \end{equation}
    is again a non-negative function of trajectories, now of length $t$. The support condition ensures that no part of the integral is lost. Since $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}$ is a function of $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ and trajectory $j$ is the ancestor of $N_{t-1}^{(j)}$ trajectories,
    \begin{align}
        E\left[\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_t^{(i)}\,\varphi(X_{0:t}^{(i)}) \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right] &= \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \bar{\varphi}\left(X_{0:t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})}\right) \\
        &= \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} N_{t-1}^{(j)}\,\bar{\varphi}(X_{0:t-1}^{(j)}).
        \label{eq:pf_propagation_step}
    \end{align}

    \emph{Resampling.} The estimator $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}$ is a function of $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{-}$, and since the resampling is independent of everything generated before it given the normalized weights, conditioning on $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{-}$ affects the offspring counts only through the normalized weights. Proposition~\ref{prop:resampling_unbiased}, applied to the trajectories $X_{0:t-1}^{(1:N)}$ with $g = \bar{\varphi}$, therefore gives
    \begin{align}
        E\left[\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}N_{t-1}^{(j)}\,\bar{\varphi}(X_{0:t-1}^{(j)}) \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{-}\right] &= \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_{t-1}^{(j)}\,\bar{\varphi}(X_{0:t-1}^{(j)}) \\
        &= \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-2}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_{t-1}^{(j)}\,\bar{\varphi}(X_{0:t-1}^{(j)}),
        \label{eq:pf_resampling_step}
    \end{align}
    where the last equality follows from
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}\,\tilde{w}_{t-1}^{(j)} = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{t-1}^{(k)}\right)\frac{\alpha_{t-1}^{(j)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{t-1}^{(k)}} = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-2}\,\frac{\alpha_{t-1}^{(j)}}{N}.
        \label{eq:pf_weight_identity}
    \end{equation}

    \emph{Conclusion.} It remains to combine the two steps. Write
    \begin{equation}
        Z_t = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_t^{(i)}\varphi(X_{0:t}^{(i)})
    \end{equation}
    for the random variable inside the expectation in~\eqref{eq:pf_unnormalized_unbiased}. Since $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{-}$ is contained in $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$, the law of total expectation allows us to average out the randomness in two stages, first the propagation at time $t$ and then the resampling at time $t-1$,
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[Z_t\right] = E\left[E\left[E\left[Z_t\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{-}\right]\right].
    \end{equation}
    The innermost conditional expectation is given by~\eqref{eq:pf_propagation_step}, and the conditional expectation of the result given $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{-}$ is given by~\eqref{eq:pf_resampling_step}. Therefore
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[Z_t\right] = E\left[\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-2}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_{t-1}^{(j)}\,\bar{\varphi}(X_{0:t-1}^{(j)})\right].
    \end{equation}
    The right-hand side has exactly the form of the left-hand side of~\eqref{eq:pf_unnormalized_unbiased}, only at time $t-1$ and with the function $\bar{\varphi}$ in place of $\varphi$. Because $\bar{\varphi}$ is non-negative, the induction hypothesis applies and gives
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[Z_t\right] = \int \bar{\varphi}(x_{0:t-1})\,p(x_{0:t-1},y_{1:t-1}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:t-1}.
    \end{equation}
    Inserting the definition of $\bar{\varphi}$ turns this into a double integral,
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[Z_t\right] = \iint \varphi(x_{0:t})\,f(y_t\mid x_t,\theta)\,p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta)\,p(x_{0:t-1},y_{1:t-1}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_t\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:t-1}.
    \end{equation}
    By Proposition~\ref{prop:joint_density}, extending the joint density by one time step multiplies it by the transition and observation densities,
    \begin{equation}
        p(x_{0:t},y_{1:t}\mid\theta) = p(x_{0:t-1},y_{1:t-1}\mid\theta)\,p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta)\,f(y_t\mid x_t,\theta),
    \end{equation}
    so the product of the three densities in the double integral is the joint density of $(x_{0:t},y_{1:t})$, and
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[Z_t\right] = \int\varphi(x_{0:t})\,p(x_{0:t},y_{1:t}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:t},
    \end{equation}
    which is the claim at time $t$. The order of integration may be exchanged because all integrands are non-negative.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:pf_marginal_likelihood_unbiased}
    Under the assumptions of Lemma~\ref{lem:pf_unnormalized_unbiased}, the particle filter marginal likelihood estimator is unbiased for every number of particles $N\ge 1$ and every $t=1,\ldots,T$,
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:t}\mid\theta)\right] = p(y_{1:t}\mid\theta).
    \end{equation}
    In particular, $E\left[\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\right] = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Take $\varphi\equiv 1$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:pf_unnormalized_unbiased}. The left-hand side of~\eqref{eq:pf_unnormalized_unbiased} becomes
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1} \, \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_t^{(i)}\right] = E\left[\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_t\right],
    \end{equation}
    and the right-hand side becomes $\int p(x_{0:t},y_{1:t}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:t} = p(y_{1:t}\mid\theta)$.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:pf_unnormalized_unbiased} is stated for functions of entire trajectories, although Theorem~\ref{thm:pf_marginal_likelihood_unbiased} only needs $\varphi\equiv 1$. The general form is used in Section~\ref{sec:pmmh_trajectories}, where the trajectories of the particle filter are used for inference about the latent states.

Unbiasedness does not carry over to the logarithm: by Jensen's inequality, $E[\log\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)] \le \log p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$. In contrast to SIS, under mild conditions the relative variance of $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ grows only linearly in $T$ for a fixed $N$ \cite{chopin2020smc}.

\subsection{Bootstrap particle filter}
\label{sec:bootstrap_particle_filter}
The simplest and most widely used particle filter is the bootstrap particle filter, which proposes from the model itself,
\begin{equation}
    q_t(x_t\mid x_{0:t-1},y_{1:t},\theta) = p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta), \qquad q_0(x_0\mid\theta) = \chi(x_0\mid\theta).
\end{equation}
The incremental weights simplify to $\alpha_0^{(i)} = 1$ and $\alpha_t^{(i)} = f(y_t\mid X_t^{(i)},\theta)$, which are strictly positive for the MSSV model, since its observation density is Gaussian. The initial weights are equal, so resampling at time $0$ is skipped by setting $A_0^{(i)} = i$, which is trivially unbiased. The marginal likelihood estimator~\eqref{eq:pf_marginal_likelihood_estimator} becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(y_t\mid X_t^{(i)},\theta)\right),
    \label{eq:bootstrap_marginal_likelihood_estimator}
\end{equation}
and it is unbiased by Theorem~\ref{thm:pf_marginal_likelihood_unbiased}. The complete procedure is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:bootstrap_particle_filter}. Its time complexity is $O(NT)$. The space complexity is $O(N)$ if only the current particles are kept, and $O(NT)$ if entire trajectories are needed. Expectations are computed from the weighted particles before resampling, since resampling only adds Monte Carlo variability.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Bootstrap particle filter}
\label{alg:bootstrap_particle_filter}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Observations $y_{1:T}$, number of particles $N$, parameters $\theta$, model densities $\chi$, $p$, $f$ and an unbiased resampling scheme.
    \STATE Sample $X_0^{(i)} \sim \chi(x_0 \mid \theta)$ and set $A_0^{(i)} = i$ for $i=1,\ldots,N$.
    \STATE Set $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_0 = 1$.
    \FOR{$t=1,\ldots,T$}
        \STATE Propagate $X_t^{(i)} \sim p(\cdot \mid X_{t-1}^{(A_{t-1}^{(i)})}, \theta)$ for $i=1,\ldots,N$.
        \STATE Compute $\alpha_t^{(i)} = f(y_t \mid X_t^{(i)}, \theta)$ for $i=1,\ldots,N$.
        \STATE Update $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_t = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{t-1}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_t^{(j)}$.
        \STATE Normalize $\tilde{w}_t^{(i)} = \alpha_t^{(i)} / \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_t^{(j)}$ for $i=1,\ldots,N$.
        \STATE Resample: draw ancestor indices $A_t^{(1:N)}$ according to $\tilde{w}_t^{(1:N)}$.
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN Particles $X_{0:T}^{(1:N)}$, normalized weights $\tilde{w}_T^{(1:N)}$ and $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Particle Markov chain Monte Carlo}
\label{sec:particle_mcmc}
So far, the model parameters $\theta$ have been assumed known. In practice they are not, and in the Bayesian framework they are treated as random variables with a prior density $p(\theta)$. The object of interest is then the posterior distribution of the parameters,
\begin{equation}
    p(\theta\mid y_{1:T}) = \frac{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(y_{1:T})} \propto p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta),
    \label{eq:parameter_posterior}
\end{equation}
where $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is the marginal likelihood from Definition~\ref{def:marginal_likelihood} and $p(y_{1:T}) = \int p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta$ is the (intractable) model evidence.
Sampling directly from~\eqref{eq:parameter_posterior} is not possible, and importance sampling in the parameter space is typically inefficient when $\theta$ is multidimensional.
The standard tool in such problems is Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), which constructs a Markov chain whose long-run behaviour is governed by the target distribution.
For state-space models, however, MCMC faces an additional obstacle: the marginal likelihood $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is itself intractable.
Particle Markov chain Monte Carlo (PMCMC) methods \cite{andrieu2010particle} resolve this by combining MCMC in the parameter space with particle filters in the state space.

As in all previous sections, all distributions in this section are described by densities. An integral over a variable with continuous and discrete components is taken with respect to the product of the Lebesgue and counting measures, and $\delta(\cdot)$ still acts as a Dirac delta in continuous components (in discrete components, it actually acts as a Kronecker delta).

\subsection{Markov chains}
\label{sec:markov_chains}
In this and the next subsection, $\theta$ denotes a variable in a generic space $\Theta$, and $\pi$ a generic probability density on $\Theta$, called the \emph{target}. The results will later be applied to an extended space that contains auxiliary variables in addition to the parameters.

\begin{definition}
    A sequence of random variables $\theta_0,\theta_1,\theta_2,\ldots$ in $\Theta$ is a \emph{Markov chain} with \emph{transition density} $K(\theta'\mid\theta)$ if, for every $m\ge 1$, the conditional density of $\theta_m$ given $\theta_{0:m-1}$ is $K(\theta_m\mid\theta_{m-1})$. Here $K(\cdot\mid\theta)$ is a probability density for every $\theta\in\Theta$, possibly containing point masses.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    The density $\pi$ is \emph{invariant} (or stationary) for the transition density $K$ if
    \begin{equation}
        \int \pi(\theta)\,K(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \pi(\theta').
        \label{eq:invariance}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
In words, if $\theta_{m-1}\sim\pi$, then also $\theta_m\sim\pi$. Verifying~\eqref{eq:invariance} directly requires evaluating an integral, which is rarely possible. A stronger, but local and therefore easily verifiable, condition is the following.

\begin{definition}
    A transition density $K$ satisfies \emph{detailed balance} with respect to $\pi$ if 
    \begin{equation}
        \pi(\theta)\,K(\theta'\mid\theta) = \pi(\theta')\,K(\theta\mid\theta').
        \label{eq:detailed_balance}
    \end{equation}

\end{definition}
Such definiton tells us that the joint density of $(\theta_{m-1},\theta_m)$ is symmetric, so the chain is reversible in time. 
    
\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:detailed_balance_invariance}
    If $K$ satisfies detailed balance with respect to $\pi$, then $\pi$ is invariant for $K$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    If we take~\eqref{eq:detailed_balance} and integrate both sides with respect to $\theta$ we get
    \begin{equation}
        \int \pi(\theta)\,K(\theta' \mid \theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \int \pi(\theta')\,K(\theta \mid \theta')\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \pi(\theta'),
    \end{equation} 
    where the last equality follows from the fact that $K(\cdot\mid\theta')$ is a probability density for every $\theta'\in\Theta$, hence it integrates to one.
\end{proof}


Invariance alone does not guarantee that the chain actually converges to $\pi$ from an arbitrary starting point. Two further properties are needed.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:irreducibility}
    A Markov chain with transition density $K$ is \emph{$\pi$-irreducible} if for every $\theta\in\Theta$ and every set $A\subseteq\Theta$ with $\int_A \pi(\theta')\,\mathrm{d}\theta'>0$ there exists an $n\ge 1$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        \int_A K^n(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta' > 0,
    \end{equation}
    where $K^n$ is the $n$-step transition density, defined recursively as
    \begin{equation}
        K^n(\theta'\mid\theta) = \int K^{n-1}(\theta'\mid\theta'')\,K(\theta''\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta'', \quad n\ge 2, \quad K^1 = K.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
In words, from any starting point, every region that carries positive target probability is reached with positive probability after finitely many steps. Without this property, the chain could remain trapped in a part of $\Theta$ and never explore the rest of the support of $\pi$.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:aperiodicity}
    A $\pi$-irreducible Markov chain with transition density $K$ is \emph{periodic} with period $d\ge 2$ if there exist disjoint sets $E_0,\ldots,E_{d-1}\subseteq\Theta$, each with positive target probability, such that
    \begin{equation}
        \int_{E_{(i+1)\bmod d}} K(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta' = 1 \quad \text{for all } \theta\in E_i,\ i=0,\ldots,d-1.
    \end{equation}
    The chain is \emph{aperiodic} if it is not periodic for any $d\ge 2$.
\end{definition}
A periodic chain cycles through the sets $E_0,E_1,\ldots,E_{d-1},E_0,\ldots$ deterministically, so the distribution of $\theta_m$ keeps oscillating and cannot converge, even though time averages may still converge. 
\begin{definition}
    \label{def:total_variation}
    The \emph{total variation distance} between two probability densities $\mu$ and $\nu$ on $\Theta$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = \sup_{A\subseteq\Theta}\left|\int_A \mu(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta - \int_A \nu(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right|.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

We state the following result without proof; see \cite[Theorems~1 and~3, Corollary~2]{tierney1994markov} and \cite[Chapter~6]{robert2004monte}.
\begin{theorem}[Ergodic theorem]
    \label{thm:ergodic_theorem}
    Let $\theta_0,\theta_1,\ldots$ be a $\pi$-irreducible Markov chain with transition density $K$ and invariant density $\pi$. Then $\pi$ is the unique invariant density, and the following hold for $\pi$-almost every starting point $\theta_0$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \emph{Law of large numbers.} For every $g$ with $E_\pi[|g(\theta)|]<\infty$,
        \begin{equation}
            \hat{I}_M^{\mathrm{MCMC}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} g(\theta_m) \xrightarrow[M\to\infty]{a.s.} E_\pi[g(\theta)].
        \end{equation}
        \item \emph{Convergence in distribution.} If the chain is in addition aperiodic, the distribution of the $m$-th state converges to $\pi$ in total variation,
        \begin{equation}
            \left\|K^m(\cdot\mid\theta_0) - \pi\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{} 0.
        \end{equation}
    \end{enumerate}
    If the chain is generated by a Metropolis-Hastings kernel (Section~\ref{sec:metropolis_hastings}), both statements hold for every starting point $\theta_0$ with $\pi(\theta_0)>0$.
\end{theorem}

The two statements concern different objects. The first is about the time average over the trajectory $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_M$ as the number of iterations $M$ grows, and it does not require aperiodicity, since averaging over a full cycle removes any periodic behaviour. The second is about the distribution of a single state $\theta_m$ as $m$ grows, and it justifies treating late states of the chain as approximate draws from $\pi$.

The MCMC estimator $\hat{I}_M^{\mathrm{MCMC}}$ plays the same role as the Monte Carlo estimator~\eqref{eq:monte_carlo_estimator}, with the important difference that the samples are now dependent and only asymptotically distributed according to $\pi$. In practice, the first iterations (the \emph{burn-in}) are therefore discarded.

\subsection{Metropolis-Hastings algorithm}
\label{sec:metropolis_hastings}
The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm \cite{metropolis1953equation,hastings1970monte} is a general recipe for constructing a transition density that satisfies detailed balance with respect to a given target. Assume that the target is known only up to a normalizing constant,
\begin{equation}
    \pi(\theta) = \frac{\tilde{\pi}(\theta)}{Z}, \qquad Z = \int \tilde{\pi}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta \in (0,\infty),
\end{equation}
where the unnormalized density $\tilde{\pi}\ge 0$ can be evaluated pointwise. The algorithm requires a \emph{proposal density} $q(\theta'\mid\theta)$ from which it is easy to sample. It is not to be confused with the importance sampling proposals of the previous sections. Likewise, the acceptance probability $\alpha(\theta,\theta')$ defined below is unrelated to the incremental weights $\alpha_t^{(i)}$.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:mh_acceptance_probability}
    The \emph{Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability} of moving from $\theta$ to $\theta'$ is
    \begin{equation}
        \alpha(\theta,\theta') = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\pi(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\right\} = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\tilde{\pi}(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\tilde{\pi}(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\right\},
        \label{eq:mh_acceptance_probability}
    \end{equation}
    with the convention $\alpha(\theta,\theta') = 1$ if $\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta) = 0$.
\end{definition}

The normalizing constant $Z$ cancels in the ratio, so, exactly as for self-normalized importance sampling, only the unnormalized density $\tilde{\pi}$ is required. The resulting procedure is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings}.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Metropolis-Hastings}
\label{alg:metropolis_hastings}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Unnormalized target density $\tilde{\pi}$, proposal density $q$, number of iterations $M$, initial state $\theta_0$ with $\tilde{\pi}(\theta_0)>0$.
    \FOR{$m=1,\ldots,M$}
        \STATE Sample $\theta' \sim q(\cdot\mid\theta_{m-1})$.
        \STATE Compute $\alpha(\theta_{m-1},\theta')$ according to~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_probability}.
        \STATE Sample $V \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$.
        \IF{$V \le \alpha(\theta_{m-1},\theta')$}
            \STATE Accept: set $\theta_m = \theta'$.
        \ELSE
            \STATE Reject: set $\theta_m = \theta_{m-1}$.
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Given $\theta$, the chain proposes a new value $\theta'$ according to $q(\cdot\mid\theta)$, and accepts it with probability $\alpha(\theta,\theta')$.
If not accepted, it stays at $\theta$, which happens with the rejection probability
\begin{equation}
    r(\theta) = 1 - \int \alpha(\theta,\theta')\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta' \in [0,1].
\end{equation}
The transition density of Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings} is therefore
\begin{equation}
    K_{\mathrm{MH}}(\theta'\mid\theta) = \alpha(\theta,\theta')\,q(\theta'\mid\theta) + r(\theta)\,\delta(\theta'-\theta).
    \label{eq:mh_kernel}
\end{equation}
The first term corresponds to accepted proposals, and the second, a point mass at the current state, to rejections. Because of this point mass, $K_{\mathrm{MH}}(\cdot\mid\theta)$ is not an ordinary density whenever $r(\theta)>0$.

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:mh_detailed_balance}
    The Metropolis-Hastings transition density~\eqref{eq:mh_kernel} satisfies detailed balance with respect to $\pi$. Consequently, $\pi$ is an invariant density of the Metropolis-Hastings chain.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We start with
    \begin{equation*}
        \pi(\theta)K_{\mathrm{MH}}(\theta' \mid \theta) = \pi(\theta)\alpha(\theta,\theta')q(\theta' \mid \theta) + \pi(\theta)r(\theta)\delta(\theta' - \theta)
    \end{equation*}
    and consider each term on its own. The first term can be expanded as
    \begin{align*}
        \pi(\theta)\alpha(\theta,\theta')q(\theta' \mid \theta) &= \pi(\theta)q(\theta' \mid \theta) \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\pi(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\right\} \\
        &= \min\left\{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta' \mid \theta), \, \pi(\theta')\,q(\theta \mid \theta')\right\} \\
        &= \pi(\theta')q(\theta \mid \theta') \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)}{\pi(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}\right\} \\
        &= \pi(\theta')\alpha(\theta',\theta)q(\theta \mid \theta').
    \end{align*}
    For the second term, we have to remember the definition of Dirac delta function, which is a generalized function that can be represented as
    \begin{equation*}
        \delta(x) = \begin{cases}
            0, \quad x \neq 0 \\
            \infty, \quad x = 0
        \end{cases} \quad \text{such that} \quad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(x)\, \mathrm{d}x = 1.
    \end{equation*}
    This means that the second term is nonzero only when $\theta' = \theta$, in which case it holds
    \begin{align*}
        \pi(\theta)r(\theta) &= \pi(\theta')r(\theta') \\
        \pi(\theta)r(\theta)\delta(\theta' - \theta) &= \pi(\theta')r(\theta')\delta(\theta - \theta')
    \end{align*}
    Together this gives us
    \begin{align*}
        \pi(\theta)K_{\mathrm{MH}}(\theta' \mid \theta) &= \pi(\theta)\alpha(\theta,\theta')q(\theta' \mid \theta) + \pi(\theta)r(\theta)\delta(\theta' - \theta) \\
        &= \pi(\theta')\alpha(\theta',\theta)q(\theta \mid \theta') + \pi(\theta')r(\theta')\delta(\theta - \theta') \\
        &= \pi(\theta')K_{\mathrm{MH}}(\theta \mid \theta'),
    \end{align*}
    which is exactly the detailed balance condition we had to prove.
\end{proof}

The theorem holds for any proposal density $q$. However, Theorem~\ref{thm:ergodic_theorem} additionally requires irreducibility and aperiodicity, and these do depend on $q$. A simple sufficient condition is that $q(\theta'\mid\theta)>0$ for all $\theta$ and all $\theta'$ with $\pi(\theta')>0$. Then $\alpha(\theta,\theta')\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)>0$ for every such $\theta'$, so every set $A$ of positive target probability satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \int_A K_{\mathrm{MH}}(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta' \ge \int_{A} \alpha(\theta,\theta')\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta' > 0,
\end{equation}
which gives $\pi$-irreducibility with $n=1$. It also rules out periodicity: for $\theta\in E_0$ in Definition~\ref{def:aperiodicity}, the chain would reach $E_0$ with positive probability, contradicting that it moves to the disjoint set $E_1$ with probability one. Beyond these qualitative properties, the choice of $q$ determines how quickly the chain explores $\Theta$. A common choice on $\Theta=\mathbb{R}^d$ is the Gaussian random walk proposal $q(\theta'\mid\theta) = \mathcal{N}(\theta';\theta,\Sigma)$, which is symmetric, $q(\theta'\mid\theta) = q(\theta\mid\theta')$, so that the proposal densities cancel in~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_probability}. When the random walk is performed on a transformed, unconstrained version $\vartheta = \tau(\theta)$ of the parameters, the target must be expressed as a density in $\vartheta$, which by the change of variables formula introduces the Jacobian factor $|\det \partial\tau^{-1}(\vartheta)/\partial\vartheta|$.

Applied to Bayesian parameter inference with target~\eqref{eq:parameter_posterior}, we have $\tilde{\pi}(\theta) = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)$ and $Z = p(y_{1:T})$, so the acceptance probability becomes
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(\theta,\theta') = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta')\,p(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\right\}.
    \label{eq:mh_acceptance_parameters}
\end{equation}
The intractable evidence $p(y_{1:T})$ cancels, but the marginal likelihood $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ does not. For the MSSV model, and for nonlinear state-space models in general, Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings} therefore cannot be implemented directly. A natural idea is to replace $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ in~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_parameters} by a particle filter estimate. Remarkably, if this is done correctly, the resulting chain still targets the exact posterior. The key property that makes this possible is that the particle filter estimator is unbiased (Theorem~\ref{thm:pf_marginal_likelihood_unbiased}).


\subsection{Pseudo-marginal Metropolis-Hastings}
\label{sec:pseudo_marginal_mh}
We now formalize what it means to replace the marginal likelihood in~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_parameters} by an estimate. Consider the following general setting \cite{andrieu2009pseudo}. Let $u$ denote a collection of \emph{auxiliary variables} in a space $\mathbb{U}$, which may have both continuous and discrete components, and let $\psi(u\mid\theta)$ be a probability density on $\mathbb{U}$ for every $\theta\in\Theta$. Let $\hat{Z}(\theta,u)\ge 0$ be a function such that
\begin{equation}
    \int \hat{Z}(\theta,u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) \qquad \text{for all } \theta\in\Theta.
    \label{eq:pseudo_marginal_unbiased}
\end{equation}
In words, if $U\sim\psi(\cdot\mid\theta)$, then $\hat{Z}(\theta,U)$ is a non-negative unbiased estimator of the marginal likelihood at $\theta$. For the particle filter, $U$ will contain all the random variables generated by one run of the filter, including the discrete ancestor indices, and $\psi(\cdot\mid\theta)$ will be their joint density. The density $\psi$ only needs to be sampled from, which amounts to running the filter. As we will see, it never needs to be evaluated.

The \emph{pseudo-marginal Metropolis-Hastings} algorithm is the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm with $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ replaced by its estimate, and it is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh}. It is crucial that the estimate associated with the current state is stored and reused in subsequent iterations, rather than recomputed.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Pseudo-marginal Metropolis-Hastings}
\label{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Prior density $p(\theta)$, proposal density $q$, auxiliary density $\psi$, estimator $\hat{Z}$, number of iterations $M$, initial state $\theta_0$.
    \STATE Sample $U_0\sim\psi(\cdot\mid\theta_0)$ and compute $\hat{Z}_0 = \hat{Z}(\theta_0,U_0)$; repeat until $p(\theta_0)\,\hat{Z}_0>0$.
    \FOR{$m=1,\ldots,M$}
        \STATE Sample $\theta'\sim q(\cdot\mid\theta_{m-1})$.
        \STATE Sample $U'\sim\psi(\cdot\mid\theta')$ and compute $\hat{Z}' = \hat{Z}(\theta',U')$.
        \STATE Compute
        \begin{equation*}
            \hat{\alpha} = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\hat{Z}'\,p(\theta')\,q(\theta_{m-1}\mid\theta')}{\hat{Z}_{m-1}\,p(\theta_{m-1})\,q(\theta'\mid\theta_{m-1})}\right\}.
        \end{equation*}
        \STATE Sample $V\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$.
        \IF{$V\le\hat{\alpha}$}
            \STATE Accept: set $(\theta_m,U_m,\hat{Z}_m) = (\theta',U',\hat{Z}')$.
        \ELSE
            \STATE Reject: set $(\theta_m,U_m,\hat{Z}_m) = (\theta_{m-1},U_{m-1},\hat{Z}_{m-1})$.
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

At first sight, Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh} appears to be merely an approximation of Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings}. The key insight is that it is an \emph{exact} Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, but on an extended space $\Theta\times\mathbb{U}$ that includes the auxiliary variables.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:extended_target}
    The \emph{extended target} is the density on $\Theta\times\mathbb{U}$ given by
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{\pi}(\theta,u) = \frac{p(\theta)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\hat{Z}(\theta,u)}{p(y_{1:T})}.
        \label{eq:extended_target}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The extended target can be read as the joint density of $(\theta,U)$ when $\theta$ is drawn from the prior and $U$ from $\psi(\cdot\mid\theta)$, reweighted by the estimate $\hat{Z}(\theta,U)$, in the same way as the posterior~\eqref{eq:parameter_posterior} is the prior reweighted by the marginal likelihood.

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:pseudo_marginal_exact}
    Assume that the estimator satisfies~\eqref{eq:pseudo_marginal_unbiased}. Then
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\bar{\pi}$ is a probability density on $\Theta\times\mathbb{U}$;
        \item the $\theta$-marginal of $\bar{\pi}$ is the posterior, $\int \bar{\pi}(\theta,u)\,\mathrm{d}u = p(\theta\mid y_{1:T})$;
        \item the sequence $(\theta_m,U_m)_{m\ge 0}$ generated by Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh} is a Metropolis-Hastings chain on $\Theta\times\mathbb{U}$ with target density $\bar{\pi}$ and proposal density
        \begin{equation}
            \bar{q}(\theta',u'\mid\theta,u) = q(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\psi(u'\mid\theta').
            \label{eq:extended_proposal}
        \end{equation}
    \end{enumerate}
    Consequently, $\bar{\pi}$ is an invariant density of $(\theta_m,U_m)_{m\ge0}$, and if $(\theta_{m-1},U_{m-1})\sim\bar{\pi}$, then $\theta_m\sim p(\theta\mid y_{1:T})$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    \emph{1 and 2.} Clearly $\bar{\pi}\ge 0$. Integrating out the auxiliary variables and using~\eqref{eq:pseudo_marginal_unbiased} gives
    \begin{align}
        \int \bar{\pi}(\theta,u)\,\mathrm{d}u &= \frac{p(\theta)}{p(y_{1:T})}\int \hat{Z}(\theta,u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u \\
        &= \frac{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(y_{1:T})} = p(\theta\mid y_{1:T}),
    \end{align}
    which proves the second claim. Integrating additionally over $\theta$ gives total mass one, which proves the first.

    \emph{3.} Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh} proposes by first drawing $\theta'\sim q(\cdot\mid\theta)$ and then $U'\sim\psi(\cdot\mid\theta')$, so the joint proposal density of $(\theta',U')$ is~\eqref{eq:extended_proposal}. It does not depend on the current auxiliary variables $u$. The Metropolis-Hastings ratio~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_probability} on the extended space is therefore
    \begin{align}
        \frac{\bar{\pi}(\theta',u')\,\bar{q}(\theta,u\mid\theta',u')}{\bar{\pi}(\theta,u)\,\bar{q}(\theta',u'\mid\theta,u)}
        &= \frac{p(\theta')\,\psi(u'\mid\theta')\,\hat{Z}(\theta',u')\;q(\theta\mid\theta')\,\psi(u\mid\theta)}{p(\theta)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\hat{Z}(\theta,u)\;q(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\psi(u'\mid\theta')} \\
        &= \frac{\hat{Z}(\theta',u')\,p(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\hat{Z}(\theta,u)\,p(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)},
    \end{align}
    since the evidence $p(y_{1:T})$ and both auxiliary densities cancel, which is exactly the ratio in $\hat{\alpha}$. The cancellation is valid because $U$ and $U'$ are drawn from $\psi(\cdot\mid\theta)$ and $\psi(\cdot\mid\theta')$, so $\psi(U\mid\theta)>0$ and $\psi(U'\mid\theta')>0$.

    By Theorem~\ref{thm:mh_detailed_balance}, $\bar{\pi}$ is invariant for $(\theta_m,U_m)_{m\ge 0}$. If $(\theta_{m-1},U_{m-1})\sim\bar{\pi}$, then $(\theta_m,U_m)\sim\bar{\pi}$, and by the second claim, $\theta_m\sim p(\theta\mid y_{1:T})$.
\end{proof}

The extended proposal~\eqref{eq:extended_proposal} is what makes the method practical. The density of the auxiliary variables appears in both the extended target and the extended proposal, so it cancels from the acceptance ratio and never has to be evaluated. This is important, since for the particle filter $\psi$ is the joint density of all the particles and ancestor indices, which is complicated and, for resampling schemes such as systematic resampling, not even available in a convenient form.

The proof also explains why the estimate of the current state must not be recomputed. Reusing $\hat{Z}_{m-1}$ corresponds to keeping $U_{m-1}$ as part of the state of the chain. Drawing a fresh $U$ for the current state at every iteration would instead change the transition density, which would then no longer satisfy detailed balance with respect to $\bar{\pi}$, and the resulting chain would, in general, not target the posterior. 
Finally, the proof shows which property of the estimator is needed. Regardless of the estimator, the chain targets the normalized version of $p(\theta)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\hat{Z}(\theta,u)$, and the $\theta$-marginal of this target is proportional to $p(\theta)\int\hat{Z}(\theta,u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u$.
It is therefore the posterior if and only if $\int\hat{Z}(\theta,u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u = c\,p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ for some constant $c>0$ that does not depend on $\theta$.
Unbiasedness~\eqref{eq:pseudo_marginal_unbiased} is the case $c=1$. For general $c$, the estimator $\hat{Z}/c$ is unbiased and gives the same acceptance ratio, so the two algorithms coincide.

\subsection{Particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings}
\label{sec:pmmh}
Particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings (PMMH) \cite{andrieu2010particle} is the pseudo-marginal Metropolis-Hastings algorithm in which the estimator is the particle filter marginal likelihood estimator~\eqref{eq:pf_marginal_likelihood_estimator}. 
In this thesis, the bootstrap particle filter of Algorithm~\ref{alg:bootstrap_particle_filter} is used. The auxiliary variables are then all the random variables generated by one run of the filter at parameter $\theta$,
\begin{equation}
    U = \left(X_{0:T}^{(1:N)},\,A_{1:T-1}^{(1:N)}\right),
\end{equation}
that is, the particles at every time step and the ancestor indices of all resampling steps that affect the estimate. The indices $A_0^{(i)} = i$ are fixed, and the indices $A_T^{(1:N)}$ are drawn after the last estimate has been computed, so neither is included. 
The space $\mathbb{U}$ thus contains both continuous and discrete components. Writing $a_{t-1}^{(1:N)}$ and $x_t^{(1:N)}$ for the values of the ancestor indices and the particles, with $a_0^{(i)} = i$, the density of $U$ is
\begin{equation}
    \psi(u\mid\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{N}\chi(x_0^{(i)}\mid\theta)\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(r\left(a_{t-1}^{(1:N)}\mid x_{t-1}^{(1:N)},\theta\right)\prod_{i=1}^{N} p\left(x_t^{(i)}\mid x_{t-1}^{(a_{t-1}^{(i)})},\theta\right)\right),
    \label{eq:pf_auxiliary_density}
\end{equation}
where $r(\cdot\mid x_{t-1}^{(1:N)},\theta)$ is the probability mass function of the ancestor indices given the normalized weights $\tilde{w}_{t-1}^{(1:N)}$, which are themselves functions of $x_{t-1}^{(1:N)}$ and $\theta$, with $r = 1$ for $t=1$. 
By Theorem~\ref{thm:pseudo_marginal_exact}, however, $\psi$ never needs to be evaluated, and~\eqref{eq:pf_auxiliary_density} only serves to show that it exists. The estimator is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{Z}(\theta,U) = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(y_t\mid X_t^{(i)},\theta)\right),
\end{equation}
as in~\eqref{eq:bootstrap_marginal_likelihood_estimator}. The complete procedure is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:pmmh}. Besides the parameters, the chain also stores one trajectory $X_{0:T}[m]$ of the latent states per iteration. These trajectories do not affect the parameter chain and are explained in Section~\ref{sec:pmmh_trajectories}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings}
\label{alg:pmmh}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Observations $y_{1:T}$, number of particles $N$, prior density $p(\theta)$, proposal density $q$, number of iterations $M$, initial state $\theta_0$ with $p(\theta_0)>0$.
    \STATE Run Algorithm~\ref{alg:bootstrap_particle_filter} at $\theta_0$ and set $\hat{Z}_0 = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta_0)$.
    \STATE Draw $B$ with $P(B=b) = \tilde{w}_T^{(b)}$ and set $X_{0:T}[0] = X_{0:T}^{(B)}$.
    \FOR{$m=1,\ldots,M$}
        \STATE Sample $\theta'\sim q(\cdot\mid\theta_{m-1})$.
        \STATE Run Algorithm~\ref{alg:bootstrap_particle_filter} at $\theta'$ and set $\hat{Z}' = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta')$.
        \STATE Draw $B'$ with $P(B'=b) = \tilde{w}_T'^{(b)}$ and set $X'_{0:T} = X_{0:T}'^{(B')}$.
        \STATE Compute
        \begin{equation*}
            \hat{\alpha} = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\hat{Z}'\,p(\theta')\,q(\theta_{m-1}\mid\theta')}{\hat{Z}_{m-1}\,p(\theta_{m-1})\,q(\theta'\mid\theta_{m-1})}\right\}.
        \end{equation*}
        \STATE Sample $V\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$.
        \IF{$V\le\hat{\alpha}$}
            \STATE Accept: set $(\theta_m,X_{0:T}[m],\hat{Z}_m) = (\theta',X'_{0:T},\hat{Z}')$.
        \ELSE
            \STATE Reject: set $(\theta_m,X_{0:T}[m],\hat{Z}_m) = (\theta_{m-1},X_{0:T}[m-1],\hat{Z}_{m-1})$.
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN Samples $(\theta_m,X_{0:T}[m])$, $m=1,\ldots,M$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:pmmh_exact}
    For every number of particles $N\ge 1$, the particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings chain of Algorithm~\ref{alg:pmmh} admits an invariant density whose $\theta$-marginal is the exact posterior $p(\theta\mid y_{1:T})$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    The estimator $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is a product of averages of non-negative observation densities and is therefore non-negative. The bootstrap particle filter satisfies the assumptions of Lemma~\ref{lem:pf_unnormalized_unbiased}, as noted in Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap_particle_filter}. By Theorem~\ref{thm:pf_marginal_likelihood_unbiased}, applied for each fixed $\theta$,
    \begin{equation}
        \int \hat{Z}(\theta,u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u = E\left[\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\right] = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta).
    \end{equation}
    Condition~\eqref{eq:pseudo_marginal_unbiased} is thus satisfied, and Algorithm~\ref{alg:pmmh} is a special case of Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh}. The claim follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:pseudo_marginal_exact}.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:pmmh_exact} shows that PMMH is not an approximation: the number of particles does not affect the invariant distribution, only how efficiently the chain explores it. The role of $N$ is best understood by noting that the acceptance ratio of PMMH equals the ideal ratio in~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_parameters} multiplied by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta')\,/\,p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta')}{\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,/\,p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)},
\end{equation}
the ratio of the relative errors of the two estimates. If the estimate at the current state happens to be much larger than the true marginal likelihood, this factor is small for most proposals, and the chain may get stuck at the same value for many iterations. A small $N$ produces noisy estimates and hence poor mixing, while a large $N$ makes every iteration expensive, since each iteration requires a full run of the particle filter at cost $O(NT)$. As $N\to\infty$, the estimates converge to the true marginal likelihood, and PMMH approaches the ideal Metropolis-Hastings algorithm with acceptance probability~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_parameters}. Balancing the two effects, \cite{doucet2015efficient} recommend choosing $N$ such that the standard deviation of $\log\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ at a central value of $\theta$, such as the posterior mean, is approximately $1.2$, and in any case between $1$ and $1.7$. By the remark after Theorem~\ref{thm:pf_marginal_likelihood_unbiased}, this standard deviation grows with $T$ for a fixed $N$, so longer time series require more particles, roughly $N\propto T$.

In practice, the acceptance ratio is computed on the logarithmic scale to avoid numerical underflow, since $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is a product of $T$ factors, each of which is an average of observation densities. 
Accordingly, the averages are computed with the log-sum-exp trick, the chain stores $\log\hat{Z}_m$, and a proposal is accepted if $\log V\le\log\hat{\alpha}$.

\subsection{Latent state estimation}
\label{sec:pmmh_trajectories}
Theorem~\ref{thm:pmmh_exact} concerns only the parameters, but for the MSSV model we are equally interested in the latent states, that is, in the joint posterior
\begin{equation}
    p(\theta,x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T}) = p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,p(\theta\mid y_{1:T}).
    \label{eq:joint_posterior}
\end{equation}
To see how the latent states can be obtained from PMMH, recall from the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:pseudo_marginal_exact} that the state of the chain at iteration $m$ is not only $\theta_m$, but the pair $(\theta_m,U_m)$, where $U_m$ are the auxiliary variables of the run of the filter that was last accepted. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pmmh} only records $\hat{Z}_m$, but $U_m$ is implicitly part of the chain. By definition, $U_m$ contains all particles $X_{0:T}^{(1:N)}$ and all ancestor indices of this run. Following the ancestor indices backwards as in~\eqref{eq:sisr_trajectory}, $U_m$ thus contains $N$ complete trajectories $X_{0:T}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{0:T}^{(N)}$, and together with $\theta_m$ it also determines their normalized weights $\tilde{w}_T^{(1:N)} = \tilde{w}_T^{(1:N)}(\theta_m,U_m)$.

At every iteration we therefore have $N$ weighted trajectories, and the question is how to extract from them a sample of the latent states. We select one trajectory according to its weight: we draw an index $B_m\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$ with
\begin{equation}
    P\left(B_m=b\mid\theta_m,U_m\right) = \tilde{w}_T^{(b)}(\theta_m,U_m),
    \label{eq:trajectory_selection}
\end{equation}
and set $X_{0:T}[m] = X_{0:T}^{(B_m)}$. The following theorem shows that this gives exact samples of the latent states.

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:pmmh_joint}
    Let $(\theta_m,U_m)_{m\ge0}$ be the PMMH chain of Algorithm~\ref{alg:pmmh}, and let $X_{0:T}[m]$ be selected from $U_m$ according to~\eqref{eq:trajectory_selection}. If $(\theta_m,U_m)\sim\bar{\pi}$, then
    \begin{equation}
        \left(\theta_m,X_{0:T}[m]\right)\sim p(\theta,x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T}),
    \end{equation}
    for every number of particles $N\ge1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Since $(\theta_m,U_m)\sim\bar{\pi}(\theta,u)$ and $B_m$ is drawn according to~\eqref{eq:trajectory_selection}, the triple $(\theta_m,U_m,B_m)$ has the density
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{\pi}(\theta,u,b) = \bar{\pi}(\theta,u)\,\tilde{w}_T^{(b)}(\theta,u) = \frac{p(\theta)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\hat{Z}(\theta,u)\,\tilde{w}_T^{(b)}(\theta,u)}{p(y_{1:T})}.
        \label{eq:extended_target_index}
    \end{equation}
    Let $g$ be any non-negative function of $(\theta,x_{0:T})$, and write $x_{0:T}^{(b)}$ for the trajectories contained in $u$. The selected trajectory is $X_{0:T}[m] = X_{0:T}^{(B_m)}$, so the expectation of $g(\theta_m,X_{0:T}[m])$ is obtained by integrating $g(\theta,x_{0:T}^{(b)})$ against the density~\eqref{eq:extended_target_index}, that is, by integrating over $\theta$ and $u$ and summing over $b$,
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[g\left(\theta_m,X_{0:T}[m]\right)\right] = \int\frac{p(\theta)}{p(y_{1:T})}\,I(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,
        \label{eq:pmmh_trajectory_expectation}
    \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation}
        I(\theta) = \int\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\hat{Z}(\theta,u)\sum_{b=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_T^{(b)}(\theta,u)\,g\left(\theta,x_{0:T}^{(b)}\right)\mathrm{d}u.
        \label{eq:pmmh_trajectory_inner}
    \end{equation}
    It remains to compute $I(\theta)$ for a fixed $\theta$. We do this in three steps.

    \emph{(a) $I(\theta)$ is an expectation over one run of the filter.} Recall that $\psi(\cdot\mid\theta)$ is the density of all random variables $U$ generated by one run of the filter at $\theta$. By the definition of the expectation, $\int h(u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u = E[h(U)]$ for $U\sim\psi(\cdot\mid\theta)$, so
    \begin{equation}
        I(\theta) = E\left[\hat{Z}(\theta,U)\sum_{b=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_T^{(b)}\,g\left(\theta,X_{0:T}^{(b)}\right)\right],
    \end{equation}
    where the expectation is over one ordinary run of the filter at $\theta$, and $X_{0:T}^{(b)}$ and $\tilde{w}_T^{(b)}$ are the trajectories and the weights of this run.

    \emph{(b) Removing the normalization of the weights.} By Definition~\ref{def:pf_marginal_likelihood_estimator}, $\hat{Z}(\theta,U) = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_T$ is the product of the averages of the incremental weights, so its last factor is $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_T^{(k)}$, that is, $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_T = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{T-1}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_T^{(k)}$. The normalized weights are $\tilde{w}_T^{(b)} = \alpha_T^{(b)}/\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_T^{(k)}$ by~\eqref{eq:sisr_normalized_weights}. In the product, the sum of the incremental weights cancels,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{Z}(\theta,U)\,\tilde{w}_T^{(b)} = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{T-1}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_T^{(k)}\right)\frac{\alpha_T^{(b)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_T^{(k)}} = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{T-1}\,\frac{\alpha_T^{(b)}}{N},
    \end{equation}
    which is the identity~\eqref{eq:pf_weight_identity} at $t=T$. Inserting this into the expectation from step (a) gives
    \begin{equation}
        I(\theta) = E\left[\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}_{T-1}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\alpha_T^{(b)}\,g\left(\theta,X_{0:T}^{(b)}\right)\right].
    \end{equation}
    The self-normalized weights, which are the source of the bias of a single run, have been replaced by the unnormalized incremental weights.

    \emph{(c) Applying the lemma.} The last expectation has exactly the form of the left-hand side of~\eqref{eq:pf_unnormalized_unbiased} at $t=T$, with the non-negative function $\varphi(x_{0:T}) = g(\theta,x_{0:T})$, in which $\theta$ is fixed. The bootstrap filter satisfies the assumptions of Lemma~\ref{lem:pf_unnormalized_unbiased}, as noted in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:pmmh_exact}, so the lemma gives
    \begin{equation}
        I(\theta) = \int g(\theta,x_{0:T})\,p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}.
    \end{equation}

    Inserting this into~\eqref{eq:pmmh_trajectory_expectation} and using Bayes' theorem, $p(\theta)\,p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)/p(y_{1:T}) = p(\theta,x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T})$, we obtain
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[g\left(\theta_m,X_{0:T}[m]\right)\right] = \iint g(\theta,x_{0:T})\,p(\theta,x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.
    \end{equation}
    Since this holds for every non-negative $g$, in particular for indicator functions of sets, $(\theta_m,X_{0:T}[m])$ is distributed according to the joint posterior.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:pmmh_joint} is not obvious. For a finite $N$, the weighted trajectories of a single run of the filter only approximate $p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)$, because the weights are self-normalized, so a trajectory selected from an ordinary run $U\sim\psi(\cdot\mid\theta)$ is not exact. However, $U_m$ is not an ordinary run. Dividing~\eqref{eq:extended_target} by $p(\theta\mid y_{1:T})$ shows that at stationarity, given $\theta_m=\theta$, the run $U_m$ has the density
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\pi}(u\mid\theta) = \frac{\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\hat{Z}(\theta,u)}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)},
    \label{eq:stationary_run_density}
\end{equation}
so the chain keeps runs with probability proportional to their likelihood estimate. As step (b) of the proof shows, this factor $\hat{Z}$ cancels the normalization of the weights, and the bias is thus corrected by the accept-reject step of the chain, not by the filter. In the extreme case $N=1$, the filter simply simulates one path from the prior dynamics, and $\hat{Z}$ is the likelihood of this path. PMMH is then an ordinary Metropolis-Hastings algorithm on $(\theta,x_{0:T})$ in which the latent states are proposed from their prior, which clearly targets the joint posterior.

As for the parameters, $N$ does not affect the distribution of the samples, only the efficiency of the chain. The selected trajectory changes only when a proposal is accepted, so with a small $N$, the samples $X_{0:T}[m]$ are strongly correlated.

Storing the whole $U_m$ at every iteration would require $O(NT)$ memory per iteration. Since $B_m$ depends only on $(\theta_m,U_m)$ and $\hat{\alpha}$ does not depend on $B_m$, the index can be drawn already when the filter is run at $\theta'$, and the selected trajectory is then accepted or rejected together with $\theta'$, as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:pmmh}. Only one trajectory is kept per iteration, and tracing it back from the stored ancestor indices costs $O(T)$. The trajectory must, however, come from the run that belongs to the current state of the chain. Running the filter again at the sampled $\theta_m$ after the chain has finished would give an ordinary run instead of~\eqref{eq:stationary_run_density}, and the samples would not be exact for a finite $N$.

The samples are used like any other MCMC output. Under the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:ergodic_theorem},
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} g\left(X_{0:T}[m]\right) \xrightarrow[M\to\infty]{a.s.} E\left[g(x_{0:T})\mid y_{1:T}\right]
    \label{eq:pmmh_state_estimator}
\end{equation}
for a fixed $N$. For example, averaging $h_t$ estimates $E[h_t\mid y_{1:T}]$, and the fraction of samples with $s_t=j$ estimates $P(s_t=j\mid y_{1:T})$. Since $\theta$ varies along the chain, these estimates take the uncertainty about the parameters into account, unlike a particle filter run at a point estimate $\hat{\theta}$, whose error moreover vanishes only as $N\to\infty$.
