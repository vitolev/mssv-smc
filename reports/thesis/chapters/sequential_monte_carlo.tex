\chapter{Sequential Monte Carlo Methods}
\label{chap:sequential_monte_carlo}

Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) methods provide a flexible framework for approximating probability distributions that evolve sequentially over time. 
They are particularly useful for inference in state-space models, where the latent states are typically not directly observed and their posterior distributions are analytically intractable. 
SMC methods approximate these distributions using a set of weighted samples, commonly referred to as particles, and update this approximation as new observations become available.

This chapter first introduces the foundations of SMC, beginning with Monte Carlo methods, importance sampling and sequential importance sampling. 
The problem of weight degeneracy is then addressed through resampling, leading to sequential importance sampling with resampling, also known as particle filtering. 
The bootstrap particle filter is presented in more detail.

Next, the chapter considers particle-based methods for Bayesian parameter inference. 
Particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings and particle Gibbs are introduced as particle Markov chain Monte Carlo methods. 
Finally, SMC$^2$ is presented as an extension that combines particle filtering with sequential inference for static parameters and latent states.

\section{Monte Carlo methods}
Monte Carlo methods approximate quantities involving a probability distribution by using a finite collection of random samples. The basic idea is simple: rather than evaluating an integral directly, we replace it by an average over samples drawn from the distribution of interest.

Until further notice, let $X$ be a random variable with probability density function $p(x)$, and let $g$ be a measurable function such that $E_p[|g(X)|] < \infty$. The quantity of interest is
\begin{equation}
    I = E_p[g(X)] = \int g(x)p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.
    \label{eq:target_expectation}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}
    Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} p(x)$. The \emph{Monte Carlo estimator} of $I$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i).
        \label{eq:monte_carlo_estimator}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Such an estimator has several desirable properties, including unbiasedness, consistency, and asymptotic normality.

\begin{proposition}
    The Monte Carlo estimator $\hat{I}_N$ is unbiased.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    The proof follows from the linearity of expectation and the fact that the samples are identically distributed.
    \begin{align}
        E_p[\hat{I}_N] &= E_p\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i)\right] \\
        &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N E_p[g(X_i)] \\
        &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N I = I.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    The Monte Carlo estimator $\hat{I}_N$ is consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Because the samples are i.i.d.\ and $E_p[|g(X)|] < \infty$, the strong law of large numbers applies, giving
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} I.
    \end{equation}
    Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, so the estimator is consistent.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    If $g(X)$ has finite variance, then the Monte Carlo estimator $\hat{I}_N$ is asymptotically normal.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    If $g(X)$ has finite variance $\sigma^2$, then the central limit theorem applies, giving
    \begin{equation}
        \sqrt{N}\left(\hat{I}_N-I\right)
        \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{D}
        \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
    \end{equation}
    which is the definition of asymptotic normality.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Empirical distribution}
\label{sec:empirical_distribution}

The Monte Carlo estimator can be interpreted as integration with respect to an empirical approximation constructed from the samples.

\begin{definition}
Given samples $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} p(x)$, the \emph{empirical distribution} associated with these samples is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_N(x)
    = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x-X_i),
    \label{eq:empirical_distribution}
\end{equation}
where $\delta(\cdot)$ denotes the Dirac delta function.
\end{definition}

The empirical distribution assigns equal weight $1/N$ to each sampled value. It therefore provides a discrete approximation to the distribution represented by $p(x)$. The expression in~\eqref{eq:empirical_distribution} should be understood through integration rather than as an ordinary probability density with respect to Lebesgue measure.

\begin{proposition}
For $g$, $\hat{I}_N$ and $\hat{p}_N$ as previously defined,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{I}_N = \int g(x)\hat{p}_N(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    \begin{align}
        \int g(x)\hat{p}_N(x)\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int g(x) \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
        &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \int g(x)\delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
        &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N g(X_i) = \hat{I}_N,
    \end{align}
    where the defining property of the Dirac delta function has been used.
\end{proof}

It is important to distinguish the empirical distribution $\hat{p}_N$ from the density $p$. Even when $p$ is continuous, $\hat{p}_N$ consists of point masses for every finite $N$ and is therefore not a pointwise approximation of $p(x)$. The approximation is instead meaningful through quantities such as integrals and expectations.

\section{Importance sampling}
\label{sec:importance_sampling}
The standard Monte Carlo method requires samples from the target distribution $p$. In many problems, however, sampling directly from $p$ is difficult or even impossible.
Importance sampling addresses this problem by introducing a second distribution from which sampling is easier.

\begin{definition}
Let $q(x)$ be a proposal distribution satisfying $q(x)>0$ whenever $p(x)>0$. 
The \emph{importance weight} associated with a point $x$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.
    \label{eq:importance_weight}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The expectation of $g$ under $p$ can then be rewritten as
\begin{align}
    I = E_p[g(X)] &= \int g(x)p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &= \int g(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &= E_q\left[g(X)w(X)\right].
\end{align}
Thus, although samples are generated from $q$, the importance weights correct for the difference between $q$ and $p$.

\begin{definition}
Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ and $w(X_1),\ldots,w(X_N)$ their corresponding importance weights.
The \emph{importance sampling estimator} of $I=E_p[g(X)]$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N g(X_i)w(X_i).
    \label{eq:importance_sampling_estimator}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:importance_sampling_unbiased}
    The importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}$ is unbiased.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    \begin{align}
        E_q[\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}] &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N E_q[g(X_i)w(X_i)] \\
        &= E_q\left[g(X)\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\right] \\
        &= \int g(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = I.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:importance_sampling_consistent}
    The importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}$ is consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Because $E_q[|g(X)w(X)|] = E_p[|g(X)|] < \infty$, the strong law of large numbers gives
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}} \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} I,
    \end{equation}
    which means that the estimator is consistent.
\end{proof}

Although the importance sampling estimator is unbiased and consistent for any proposal distribution satisfying the support condition, its variance depends strongly on the choice of $q$.

\subsection{(Weighted) empirical distribution}
\label{sec:weighted_empirical_distribution}

There are two equivalent ways to interpret the importance sampling estimator.
The first one follows the same logic as in Section~\ref{sec:empirical_distribution}:
\begin{align}
    \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w(X_i)g(X_i) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \int w(x)g(x) \delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &=\int w(x)g(x) \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &=\int w(x)g(x) \hat{q}_N(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,
\end{align}
where $\hat{q}_N$ is the empirical distribution of the samples $X_1,\ldots,X_N\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}q$, so the estimator is simply the expectation of $w(X)g(X)$ with respect to $\hat{q}_N$.

There is another interpretation that is particularly useful. Instead of viewing the weights as part of the integrand, we can absorb them into the empirical distribution itself.
\begin{align}
    \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w(X_i)g(X_i) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w(X_i) \int g(x) \delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &=\int g(x) \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w(X_i)\delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
    &=\int g(x) \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,
\end{align}
leading us to next definition.
\begin{definition}
Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ and $w(X_1),\ldots,w(X_N)$ their corresponding importance weights. 
The \emph{weighted empirical distribution} is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}(x) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N w(X_i)\delta(x-X_i).
    \label{eq:weighted_empirical_distribution}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

This provides an alternative interpretation of importance sampling:
although the samples are drawn from the proposal distribution $q$, the
importance weights allow the samples to represent the target distribution
$p$. Unlike ordinary Monte Carlo, where every sample contributes the same
mass $1/N$, importance sampling assigns the sample $X_i$ a mass
proportional to $w(X_i)$.

The weighted empirical distribution in
\eqref{eq:weighted_empirical_distribution}, however, is not generally a
probability distribution for a finite sample. Its total mass is
\begin{equation}
    \int \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N w(X_i),
\end{equation}
which is a random quantity and is not necessarily equal to one.
Nevertheless, the total mass has expectation equal to one. Since $X\sim q$,
\begin{align}
    E_q[w(X)] &= \int \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1, \\
    E_q\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N w(X_i)\right] &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N E_q[w(X_i)] = 1.
\end{align}
Additionally, the strong law of large numbers gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N w(X_i) \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} E_q[w(X)] = 1.
\end{equation}
Thus, although the weighted empirical distribution does not necessarily
have unit mass for a finite sample, its total mass converges almost surely
to one as the number of samples increases.

\section{Self-normalized importance sampling}
\label{sec:self_normalized_importance_sampling}
The previous construction suggests a natural modification: instead of allowing the weights to have a random total mass, normalize them. Writing $w_i = w(X_i)$ for brevity, the \emph{normalized importance weights} are
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{w}_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} w_j},
\end{equation}
which leads to the following definition.
\begin{definition}
    Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ and $\tilde{w}_1,\ldots,\tilde{w}_N$ their corresponding normalized importance weights.
    The \emph{self-normalized weighted empirical distribution} is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_i \delta(x-X_i).
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
This distribution clearly has a total mass of one.
\begin{definition}
    Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ and $w_1,\ldots,w_N$ their corresponding importance weights.
    The \emph{self-normalized importance sampling estimator} of $I=E_p[g(X)]$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i)w_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    For $g$, $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ and $\hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ as previously defined,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}} = \int g(x) \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}(x)\, \mathrm{d}x.
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    \begin{align}
        \int g(x) \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}(x)\, \mathrm{d}x &= \int g(x) \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_i \delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
        &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_i \int g(x) \delta(x-X_i)\,\mathrm{d}x \\
        &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_i g(X_i) \\
        &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i)w_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i}.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

There is a fundamental difference between $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}$ and $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$. The ordinary importance sampling estimator is unbiased, whereas the self-normalized estimator is generally biased for finite $N$.
\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_biased}
    The self-normalized importance sampling estimator is, in general, biased.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    In general, the expected value of a ratio is not equal to the ratio of expected values, so
    \begin{align}
        E_q\left[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(X_i)w_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i}\right] &\neq \frac{E_q\left[\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}g(X_i)w_i\right]}{E_q\left[\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\right]}, \\
        E_q\left[\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}\right] &\neq \frac{E_q\left[\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}\right]}{1} = I.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_consistent}
    The self-normalized importance sampling estimator is consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    The strong law of large numbers gives
    \begin{align}
        \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i)w_i &\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} I,\\
        \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i &\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} 1.
    \end{align}
    Consequently,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}} = \frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_i)w_i}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i} \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} I.
    \end{equation}
\end{proof}

At first, it may seem pointless to use the self-normalized estimator merely to ensure that $\hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ is a proper probability distribution, since the estimator loses its unbiasedness. 
However, in many applications, especially in Bayesian inference, the target density $p(x)$ is known only up to a proportionality constant. Suppose that the target has the form
\begin{equation}
    p(x) = \frac{\tilde{p}(x)}{Z},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{p}(x)$ is known and $Z$ is an intractable normalization constant. The importance weights are then
\begin{equation}
    w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \frac{\tilde{p}(x)}{Zq(x)},
\end{equation}
which makes it impossible to compute the ordinary importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}$. However, the normalized weights are still computable:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{w}_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j=1}^N w_j} = \frac{\frac{\tilde{p}(X_i)}{Zq(X_i)}}{\sum_{j=1}^N \frac{\tilde{p}(X_j)}{Zq(X_j)}} = \frac{\frac{\tilde{p}(X_i)}{q(X_i)}}{\sum_{j=1}^N \frac{\tilde{p}(X_j)}{q(X_j)}}.
\end{equation}
Therefore, the self-normalized importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ can be used even when the target density is known only up to a constant of proportionality, at the cost of introducing bias.

\section{Sequential importance sampling}
Sequential importance sampling (SIS) applies importance sampling to a sequence of random variables by exploiting the sequential structure of the problem.
Because the main focus of this thesis is on state-space models, in particular the MSSV model, we consider sequential importance sampling in the context of state-space models.

For the moment, assume that the model parameters $\theta$ are known. Given the observations $y_{1:T}$, the objective is to approximate the posterior distribution of the latent states, $p(x_{0:T} \mid y_{1:T},\theta)$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:joint_density}, the joint density is
\begin{equation}
p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \chi(x_0\mid\theta) \prod_{t=1}^T f(y_t\mid x_t,\theta) p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta).
\end{equation}
For the MSSV model, the latent state $x_t = (h_t,s_t)$ has a continuous component $h_t$ and a discrete component $s_t \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$. All densities of the latent states are therefore understood with respect to the product of the Lebesgue measure and the counting measure, so that integrals over $x_t$ are shorthand for
\begin{equation}
    \int g(x_t)\,\mathrm{d}x_t = \sum_{s_t=1}^{K} \int g(h_t,s_t)\,\mathrm{d}h_t.
\end{equation}
Correspondingly, $\delta(x_t - X_t)$ acts as a Dirac delta in $h_t$ and as a Kronecker delta in $s_t$. With this convention, all results of this chapter apply to the MSSV model without change.

Until further notice, assume that the proposal distribution can also be factorized sequentially as
\begin{equation}
q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta) = q_0(x_0\mid\theta) \prod_{t=1}^T q_t(x_t\mid x_{0:t-1},y_{1:t},\theta),
\end{equation}
from which we can sample entire trajectories of the latent states, conditioned on the currently available observations, in a sequential manner.

The importance weight can therefore be computed recursively as
\begin{align}
    w_T &= \frac{p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}{q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)} \\
    &= \frac{\chi(x_0\mid\theta) \prod_{t=1}^T f(y_t\mid x_t,\theta) p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta)}{q_0(x_0\mid\theta) \prod_{t=1}^T q_t(x_t\mid x_{0:t-1},y_{1:t},\theta)} \\
    &= w_{T-1} \cdot \frac{f(y_T\mid x_T,\theta) p(x_T\mid x_{T-1},\theta)}{q_T(x_T\mid x_{0:T-1},y_{1:T},\theta)} \\
    &= w_{T-1} \alpha_T = \prod_{t=0}^{T} \alpha_t
\end{align}
with $\alpha_0 = w_0 = \frac{\chi(x_0\mid\theta)}{q_0(x_0\mid\theta)}$.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:marginal_likelihood}
    The \emph{marginal likelihood} is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \int p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
Introducing the importance weights, the marginal likelihood can therefore be written as
\begin{equation}
    p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \int w_T\, q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} = E_q[w_T].
\end{equation}
For nonlinear and non-Gaussian state-space models, the marginal likelihood is typically intractable and therefore has to be estimated.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:marginal_likelihood_estimator}
    Let $X_{0:T}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{0:T}^{(N)} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ be sampled trajectories and $w_T^{(i)}$ their corresponding importance weights.
    Then the \emph{marginal likelihood estimator} is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_T^{(i)}.
        \label{eq:marginal_likelihood_estimator}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

As a result the marginal likelihood estimator can therefore be written as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \prod_{t=0}^{T} \alpha_t^{(i)}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:marginal_likelihood_estimator_unbiased_consistent}
    The marginal likelihood estimator $\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is unbiased and consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Unbiasedness follows from
    \begin{align}
        E_q[\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)] &= E_q\left[\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_T^{(i)}\right] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} E_q[w_T^{(i)}] \\
        &= E_q[w_T] = \int w_T q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} \\
        &= \int p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta),
    \end{align}
    where the linearity of expectation and the fact that the trajectories are identically distributed have been used.
    Since the weights $w_T^{(i)}$ are i.i.d., non-negative and have finite expectation $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$, consistency follows from the strong law of large numbers,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.} p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta).
    \end{equation}
    Alternatively, $\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is the (ordinary) importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{IS}}$ with $g\equiv 1$, hence the proofs of Propositions~\ref{prop:importance_sampling_unbiased} and~\ref{prop:importance_sampling_consistent} carry over directly.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Smoothing distribution}
\label{sec:smoothing_distribution}

\begin{definition}
    The \emph{smoothing distribution} is the posterior distribution of the entire latent trajectory given all available observations,
    \begin{equation}
        p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta) = \frac{p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}.
    \end{equation}
    The \emph{smoothing expectation} of a function $g$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        I^{\mathrm{smth}} = E\left[g(X_{0:T}) \mid y_{1:T},\theta\right] = \int g(x_{0:T}) p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Rewriting the smoothing expectation in terms of the proposal distribution gives
\begin{align}
    I^{\mathrm{smth}} &= \frac{1}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)} \int g(x_{0:T}) \frac{p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}{q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)} q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} \\
    &= \frac{E_q\left[g(X_{0:T})w_T\right]}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}
    = \frac{E_q\left[g(X_{0:T})w_T\right]}{E_q\left[w_T\right]}.
\end{align}
The smoothing distribution is thus a target known only up to the normalizing constant $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$, with unnormalized density $p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$, which is exactly the setting of Section~\ref{sec:self_normalized_importance_sampling}. Replacing both expectations by Monte Carlo averages over the sampled trajectories motivates the following definitions.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:smoothing_estimator}
    Let $X_{0:T}^{(i)} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ be sampled trajectories, $w_T^{(i)}$ their importance weights, and
    \begin{equation}
        \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} = \frac{w_T^{(i)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} w_T^{(j)}}
    \end{equation}
    the corresponding normalized weights. The \emph{smoothing estimator} of $I^{\mathrm{smth}}$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_{0:T}^{(i)}) w_T^{(i)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_T^{(i)}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} g(X_{0:T}^{(i)}),
        \label{eq:smoothing_estimator}
    \end{equation}
    inducing the \emph{empirical smoothing distribution} as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \delta(x_{0:T}-X_{0:T}^{(i)}).
        \label{eq:empirical_smoothing_distribution}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:smoothing_estimator}
    For $g$, $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$ and $\hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$ as previously defined,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}} = \int g(x_{0:T}) \hat{p}_N(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}.
    \end{equation}
    Moreover, the smoothing estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$ is, in general, biased, but consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    The first claim follows from the defining property of the Dirac delta function,
    \begin{align}
        \int g(x_{0:T}) \hat{p}_N(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \int g(x_{0:T}) \delta(x_{0:T}-X_{0:T}^{(i)})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} \\
        &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} g(X_{0:T}^{(i)}) = \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}.
    \end{align}
    The estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$ is the self-normalized importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ with target $p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)$, unnormalized target $p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$, normalizing constant $Z = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ and proposal $q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)$. 
    Bias and consistency therefore follow from Propositions~\ref{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_biased} and~\ref{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_consistent}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Filtering distribution}
\label{sec:filtering_distribution}

\begin{definition}
    The \emph{filtering distribution} is the marginal of the smoothing distribution,
    \begin{equation}
        p(x_T \mid y_{1:T},\theta) = \int p(x_{0:T} \mid y_{1:T},\theta) \,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T-1}.
    \end{equation}
    The \emph{filtering expectation} of a function $g$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        I^{\mathrm{filt}} = E\left[g(X_T) \mid y_{1:T},\theta\right] = \int g(x_T)p(x_T \mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_T.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

In the empirical setting, the states at previous times are easily integrated out, since $\delta(x_{0:T}-X_{0:T}^{(i)}) = \delta(x_T-X_T^{(i)})\,\delta(x_{0:T-1}-X_{0:T-1}^{(i)})$,
\begin{align}
    \int \hat{p}_N(x_{0:T} &\mid y_{1:T}, \theta) \,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T-1} \\
    &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \delta(x_T-X_T^{(i)}) \int \delta(x_{0:T-1}-X_{0:T-1}^{(i)}) \,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T-1} \\
    &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \delta(x_T-X_T^{(i)}),
\end{align}
which leads to the following definition.

\begin{definition}
    Let $X_{0:T}^{(i)} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} q$ and $\tilde{w}_T^{(i)}$ their corresponding normalized weights. 
    The \emph{filtering estimator} of $I^{\mathrm{filt}}$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_{T}^{(i)} g(X_T^{(i)}),
    \end{equation}
    inducing the \emph{empirical filtering distribution} is as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}(x_T \mid y_{1:T},\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \delta(x_T-X_T^{(i)}).
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:filtering_estimator}
    The filtering estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}$ is, in general, biased, but consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Define the function $\bar{g}(x_{0:T}) = g(x_T)$, which depends on the trajectory only through its final state. Its smoothing expectation is
    \begin{align}
        E\left[\bar{g}(X_{0:T}) \mid y_{1:T},\theta\right] &= \int g(x_T)\, p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T} \\
        &= \int g(x_T) \left(\int p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T-1}\right)\mathrm{d}x_T \\
        &= \int g(x_T)\, p(x_T\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_T = I^{\mathrm{filt}},
    \end{align}
    where the previous states have been integrated out. Likewise, the smoothing estimator of this expectation is
    \begin{equation}
        \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \bar{g}(X_{0:T}^{(i)}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} g(X_T^{(i)}) = \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}.
    \end{equation}
    Thus, $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}$ is the smoothing estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$ applied to $\bar{g}$, and its bias and consistency follow from Proposition~\ref{prop:smoothing_estimator}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Predictive distribution}
\label{sec:predictive_distribution}

\begin{definition}
    The (one-step) \emph{predictive distribution} is obtained by propagating the filtering distribution through the transition density,
    \begin{equation}
        p(x_{T+1} \mid y_{1:T}, \theta) = \int p(x_{T+1} \mid x_T, \theta)p(x_T \mid y_{1:T}, \theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_T.
    \end{equation}
    The \emph{predictive expectation} of a function $g$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        I^{\mathrm{pred}} = E\left[g(X_{T+1}) \mid y_{1:T}, \theta\right] = \int g(x_{T+1})p(x_{T+1} \mid y_{1:T}, \theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{T+1}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Replacing the filtering distribution with its empirical approximation gives
\begin{equation}
    \int p(x_{T+1} \mid x_T, \theta) \hat{p}_N(x_T \mid y_{1:T}, \theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_T = \sum_{i=1}^N \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} p(x_{T+1} \mid X_T^{(i)}, \theta),
\end{equation}
a mixture of transition densities weighted by the normalized weights. A particle approximation of this mixture is obtained by propagating each particle through its transition density, which leads to the following definition.

\begin{definition}
    Let $X_{0:T}^{(i)}$ and $\tilde{w}_T^{(i)}$ be as in Definition~\ref{def:smoothing_estimator}, and let $X_{T+1}^{(i)} \sim p(x_{T+1} \mid X_T^{(i)}, \theta)$ be sampled independently. 
    The \emph{predictive estimator} of $I^{\mathrm{pred}}$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_{T}^{(i)} g(X_{T+1}^{(i)}),
        \label{eq:predictive_estimator}
    \end{equation}
    inducing the \emph{empirical predictive distribution} is as
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{p}_N(x_{T+1} \mid y_{1:T}, \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} \delta(x_{T+1}-X_{T+1}^{(i)}).
        \label{eq:empirical_predictive_distribution}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:predictive_estimator}
    The predictive estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}}$ is, in general, biased, but consistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    The extended trajectories $X_{0:T+1}^{(i)}$ are independent draws from the extended proposal
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{q}(x_{0:T+1}\mid y_{1:T},\theta) = q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,p(x_{T+1}\mid x_T,\theta).
    \end{equation}
    Take as the target the extended smoothing distribution
    \begin{equation}
        p(x_{0:T+1}\mid y_{1:T},\theta) = \frac{p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(x_{T+1}\mid x_T,\theta)}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)},
    \end{equation}
    whose marginal in $x_{T+1}$ is the predictive distribution, so that
    \begin{equation}
        I^{\mathrm{pred}} = \int g(x_{T+1})\,p(x_{0:T+1}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T+1}.
    \end{equation}
    The transition densities cancel in the corresponding unnormalized importance weight,
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{w}_{T+1} = \frac{p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(x_{T+1}\mid x_T,\theta)}{q(x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,p(x_{T+1}\mid x_T,\theta)} = w_T.
    \end{equation}
    Rewriting the predictive expectation in terms of the extended proposal therefore gives
    \begin{align}
        I^{\mathrm{pred}} &= \frac{1}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)} \int g(x_{T+1})\, \bar{w}_{T+1}\, \bar{q}(x_{0:T+1}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T+1} \\
        &= \frac{E_{\bar{q}}\left[g(X_{T+1})\,w_T\right]}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}.
    \end{align}
    Analogously, the normalizing constant is
    \begin{equation}
        p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \int \bar{w}_{T+1}\, \bar{q}(x_{0:T+1}\mid y_{1:T},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T+1} = E_{\bar{q}}\left[w_T\right],
    \end{equation}
    which yields
    \begin{equation}
        I^{\mathrm{pred}} = \frac{E_{\bar{q}}\left[g(X_{T+1})\,w_T\right]}{E_{\bar{q}}\left[w_T\right]}.
    \end{equation}
    Replacing both expectations by Monte Carlo averages over $X_{0:T+1}^{(i)} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \bar{q}$ gives
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_{T+1}^{(i)})\, w_T^{(i)}}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_T^{(i)}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_T^{(i)} g(X_{T+1}^{(i)}) = \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}}.
    \end{equation}
    Hence $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}}$ is exactly the self-normalized importance sampling estimator $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{SNIS}}$ of $I^{\mathrm{pred}}$ with proposal $\bar{q}$, unnormalized target $p(x_{0:T},y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(x_{T+1}\mid x_T,\theta)$ and normalizing constant $Z = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$.
    Bias and consistency follow from Propositions~\ref{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_biased} and~\ref{prop:self_normalized_importance_sampling_consistent}.
\end{proof}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Sequential importance sampling}
\label{alg:sequential_importance_sampling}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Observations $y_{1:T}$, number of particles $N$, proposal densities $q_0,q_t$, and model densities $\chi,p,f$.

    \FOR{$i=1,\ldots,N$}
        \STATE Sample $X_0^{(i)} \sim q_0(x_0 \mid \theta)$.
        \STATE Set $w_0^{(i)} = \dfrac{\chi(X_0^{(i)}\mid \theta)}{q_0(X_0^{(i)} \mid \theta)}$.
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Normalize weights to obtain $\tilde{w}_0^{(i)}$.

    \FOR{$t=1,\ldots,T$}
        \FOR{$i=1,\ldots,N$}
            \STATE Propagate $X_t^{(i)} \sim q_t(\cdot\mid X_{0:t-1}^{(i)},y_{1:t},\theta)$.
            \STATE Compute $\alpha_t^{(i)} = \dfrac{f(y_t\mid X_t^{(i)}, \theta)p(X_t^{(i)}\mid X_{t-1}^{(i)}, \theta)}{q_t(X_t^{(i)}\mid X_{0:t-1}^{(i)},y_{1:t}, \theta)}$.
            \STATE Update $w_t^{(i)} = w_{t-1}^{(i)}\alpha_t^{(i)}$.
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE Normalize weights to obtain $\tilde{w}_t^{(i)}$.
    \ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
In summary, the sequential updates of the importance weights together with the propagation of particles allow us to approximate the smoothing, filtering, and predictive distributions, and their expectations, in a sequential manner. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:sequential_importance_sampling}.
Since all of $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{smth}}$, $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{filt}}$ and $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{pred}}$ are self-normalized, they are biased but consistent estimators. Only the marginal likelihood estimator $\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is unbiased.

The main limitation of SIS is \emph{weight degeneracy}. As the number of observations increases, the importance weights become increasingly unequal, because only a small fraction of the particle trajectories are able to explain all of the observations. Eventually, almost all of the normalized weight is concentrated on a few particles, and the remaining particles contribute virtually nothing to the estimates. This motivates the idea of resampling.

\section{Sequential importance sampling with resampling}
\label{sec:sequential_importance_sampling_with_resampling}
Sequential importance sampling with resampling (SISR), also known as particle filtering, is an extension of SIS that incorporates a resampling step to mitigate the problem of weight degeneracy. 

\begin{definition}
    The \emph{effective sample size} at time $t$ is calculated as
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{ESS}_t = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(\tilde{w}_t^{(i)}\right)^2},
    \end{equation}
    where $\tilde{w}_t^{(i)}$ are the current normalized importance weights.
\end{definition}

Resampling can be performed adaptively, only when the effective sample size falls below a chosen threshold. 
Note that $1 \le \mathrm{ESS}_t \le N$, with $\mathrm{ESS}_t = N$ for equal weights and $\mathrm{ESS}_t = 1$ when all the weight is concentrated on a single particle. In this thesis, however, resampling is performed at every time step.

Mathematically, resampling replaces the empirical distribution
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_N(x_{0:t}\mid y_{1:t}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_t^{(i)} \delta(x_{0:t}-X_{0:t}^{(i)})
\end{equation}
with a new empirical distribution
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_N^{\mathrm{res}}(x_{0:t}\mid y_{1:t}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} N^{(i)} \delta(x_{0:t}-X_{0:t}^{(i)}),
\end{equation}
where $N^{(i)}$ is the number of offspring of particle $X_{0:t}^{(i)}$ after resampling, such that $\sum_{i=1}^{N} N^{(i)} = N$. Common resampling algorithms include multinomial, stratified, residual, and systematic resampling. The last of these, systematic resampling, is used in this thesis.

The validity of resampling can be established by considering its effect on estimates of expectations with respect to the weighted empirical distribution. As always let $g$ be a measurable function with finite expectation. Before resampling, its expectation under the weighted empirical distribution is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{I}_N = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{w}_t^{(i)} g(X_{0:t}^{(i)}).
\end{equation}
After resampling, the corresponding estimate becomes
\begin{equation}
    \hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{res}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} N^{(i)}g(X_{0:t}^{(i)}).
\end{equation}
Although $\hat{I}_N$ and $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{res}}$ generally differ for a particular resampling outcome, an unbiased resampling scheme satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:unbiased_resampling}
    E\left[N^{(i)}\mid \tilde{w}_t^{(1:N)}\right] = N\tilde{w}_t^{(i)}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,N,
\end{equation}
where the expectation is taken with respect to the randomness introduced by resampling. All four schemes mentioned above satisfy this condition.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:resampling_unbiased}
    Let $\hat{I}_N$ and $\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{res}}$ be the estimators before and after resampling. For an unbiased resampling scheme satisfying~\eqref{eq:unbiased_resampling}, the resampled estimator is conditionally unbiased,
    \begin{equation}
        E\left[\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{res}} \mid \tilde{w}_t^{(1:N)}\right] = \hat{I}_N,
    \end{equation}
    where the expectation is taken with respect to the randomness introduced by resampling.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Since the resampling process does not depend on the particles, the values $g(X_{0:t}^{(i)})$ can be taken out of the conditional expectation, giving
    \begin{align}
        E\left[\hat{I}_N^{\mathrm{res}} \mid \tilde{w}_t^{(1:N)}\right] &= E\left[\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} N^{(i)}g(X_{0:t}^{(i)}) \,\middle|\, \tilde{w}_t^{(1:N)}\right] \\
        &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_{0:t}^{(i)}) E\left[N^{(i)} \mid \tilde{w}_t^{(1:N)}\right] \\
        &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_{0:t}^{(i)}) N\tilde{w}_t^{(i)} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} g(X_{0:t}^{(i)}) \tilde{w}_t^{(i)} = \hat{I}_N.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}

Resampling creates a new set of equally weighted particles. A slight complication arises when keeping track of the entire trajectories of the latent states, as resampling mixes the trajectories of the particles. This can be solved by keeping track of the ancestry of the particles, which is done by storing the indices of the parent particles during resampling. This allows us to reconstruct the entire trajectory of each particle at any time step.
Figure~\ref{fig:resampling} illustrates the effect of resampling on the particle trajectories. The resampling step prunes particles with low weights and replicates particles with high weights, so that computational effort is concentrated on regions of high posterior probability.
A side effect of repeated resampling is \emph{path degeneracy}: going backwards in time, the reconstructed trajectories coalesce to only a few common ancestors, so the approximation of the smoothing distribution $p(x_{0:t}\mid y_{1:t})$ deteriorates for early states, even though the approximation of the filtering distribution remains accurate.

\subsection{Bootstrap particle filter}
One of the simplest and most widely used particle filtering algorithms is the bootstrap particle filter, which specifies the proposal distribution as
\begin{equation}
    q_t(x_t\mid x_{0:t-1},y_{1:t},\theta) = p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta), \qquad q_0(x_0\mid\theta) = \chi(x_0\mid\theta),
\end{equation}
where $p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta)$ and $\chi(x_0\mid\theta)$ are the transition and initial densities of the state-space model. The weight update then simplifies to
\begin{equation}
    w_t = w_{t-1} \frac{f(y_t\mid x_t,\theta)p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\theta)}{q_t(x_t\mid x_{0:t-1},y_{1:t},\theta)} = w_{t-1} f(y_t\mid x_t,\theta).
\end{equation}
The entire bootstrap particle filter algorithm is presented in Algorithm~\ref{alg:bootstrap_particle_filter}.
Due to the resampling step, the particles at the end of each iteration become equally weighted.
Expectations with respect to the empirical distribution can be calculated either by using the weighted particles before resampling or by using the equally weighted particles after resampling. The latter introduces additional Monte Carlo variability due to the randomness of the resampling step, so the former is generally preferred.
The time complexity of the bootstrap particle filter is $O(N)$ per time step, since the weight update and resampling steps each take $O(N)$ time. In total, the time complexity is $O(NT)$.
Similarly, the space complexity is $O(N)$ per time step, since for every time step, we need to store $N$ particles and their weights. But if we want entire trajectories, the space complexity is $O(NT)$, since we need to storeparticles for each time step.
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Bootstrap particle filter}
\label{alg:bootstrap_particle_filter}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Observations $y_{1:T}$, number of particles $N$, model densities $\chi$, $p$, $f$.

    \FOR{$i=1,\ldots,N$}
        \STATE Sample $X_0^{(i)} \sim \chi(x_0 \mid \theta)$.
        \STATE Set $w_0^{(i)} = \tilde{w}_0^{(i)} = 1/N$.
    \ENDFOR

    \FOR{$t=1,\ldots,T$}
        \FOR{$i=1,\ldots,N$}
            \STATE Propagate $X_t^{(i)} \sim p(\cdot \mid X_{t-1}^{(i)}, \theta)$.
            \STATE Compute $\alpha_t^{(i)} = f(y_t \mid X_t^{(i)}, \theta)$. 
            \STATE Update $w_t^{(i)} = w_{t-1}^{(i)} \alpha_t^{(i)}$.
        \ENDFOR
        \FOR{$i=1,\ldots,N$}
            \STATE Normalize weights $\tilde{w}_t^{(i)} = \dfrac{w_t^{(i)}}{\sum_{j=1}^N w_t^{(j)}}$.
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE Resample particles $\{X_t^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ according to  $\{\tilde{w}_t^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$.
        \STATE Set $w_t^{(i)} = 1/N$ for all $i=1,\ldots,N$.
    \ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Particle Markov chain Monte Carlo}
\label{sec:particle_mcmc}
So far, the model parameters $\theta$ have been assumed known. In practice they are not, and in the Bayesian framework they are treated as random variables with a prior density $p(\theta)$. The object of interest is then the posterior distribution of the parameters,
\begin{equation}
    p(\theta\mid y_{1:T}) = \frac{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(y_{1:T})} \propto p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta),
    \label{eq:parameter_posterior}
\end{equation}
where $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is the marginal likelihood from Definition~\ref{def:marginal_likelihood} and $p(y_{1:T}) = \int p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta$ is the (intractable) model evidence.
The standard tool in such problems is Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), which constructs a Markov chain whose long-run behaviour is governed by the target distribution.
For state-space models, however, MCMC faces an additional obstacle: the marginal likelihood $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is itself intractable.
Particle Markov chain Monte Carlo (PMCMC) methods \cite{andrieu2010particle} resolve this by combining MCMC in the parameter space with particle filters in the state space.

\subsection{Markov chains}
\label{sec:markov_chains}
Throughout this section, let $\pi(\theta)$ denote a target density on the parameter space $\Theta$. As in Section~\ref{sec:self_normalized_importance_sampling}, the target is typically known only up to a normalizing constant, $\pi(\theta) = \tilde{\pi}(\theta)/Z$, where $\tilde{\pi}$ can be evaluated pointwise.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:transition_kernel}
    Let $(\Theta,\mathcal{B}(\Theta))$ be a measurable space. A \emph{transition kernel} on $\Theta$ is a map $K\colon \mathcal{B}(\Theta)\times\Theta\to[0,1]$, such that
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item for every $\theta\in\Theta$, the map $A\mapsto K(A\mid\theta)$ is a probability measure on $(\Theta,\mathcal{B}(\Theta))$;
        \item for every $A\in\mathcal{B}(\Theta)$, the map $\theta\mapsto K(A\mid\theta)$ is measurable.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
In other words, a transition kernel is a probability kernel from $\Theta$ to itself. The first condition says that $K(\cdot\mid\theta)$ is a probability distribution over the next state given the current state $\theta$. This distribution may contain point masses, as is the case for the Metropolis-Hastings kernel below. The second condition ensures that quantities such as $\int K(A\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta$ and $P(\theta_m\in A)$ are well defined. With a slight abuse of notation, we write $K(\theta'\mid\theta)$ for the density of $K(\cdot\mid\theta)$. A Markov chain is the sequence obtained by applying the kernel repeatedly.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:markov_chain}
    Let $K$ be a transition kernel on $\Theta$. A sequence of random variables $\theta_0,\theta_1,\theta_2,\ldots$ taking values in $\Theta$ is a \emph{Markov chain} with transition kernel $K$ if, for every $m\ge 1$,
    \begin{equation}
        \theta_m \mid \theta_{0:m-1} \sim K(\cdot\mid\theta_{m-1}).
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
In words, the distribution of $\theta_m$ depends only on the previous state $\theta_{m-1}$.

\begin{definition}
    A distribution $\pi$ is \emph{invariant} (or stationary) for the transition kernel $K$ if
    \begin{equation}
        \int \pi(\theta)\,K(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \pi(\theta') \quad \text{for all } \theta'\in\Theta.
        \label{eq:invariance}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
Hence, if $\theta_{m-1}\sim\pi$, then also $\theta_m\sim\pi$. Verifying~\eqref{eq:invariance} directly requires evaluating an integral, which is rarely possible. A stronger, but local and therefore easily verifiable, condition is the following.

\begin{definition}
    A transition kernel $K$ satisfies \emph{detailed balance} with respect to $\pi$ if
    \begin{equation}
        \pi(\theta)\,K(\theta'\mid\theta) = \pi(\theta')\,K(\theta\mid\theta') \quad \text{for all } \theta,\theta'\in\Theta.
        \label{eq:detailed_balance}
    \end{equation}
    A Markov chain whose kernel satisfies detailed balance with respect to $\pi$ is called \emph{reversible} with respect to $\pi$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:detailed_balance_invariance}
    If $K$ satisfies detailed balance with respect to $\pi$, then $\pi$ is invariant for $K$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Integrating both sides of~\eqref{eq:detailed_balance} with respect to $\theta$ gives
    \begin{equation}
        \int \pi(\theta)\,K(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \pi(\theta') \int K(\theta\mid\theta')\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \pi(\theta'),
    \end{equation}
    since $K(\cdot\mid\theta')$ is a probability distribution.
\end{proof}

Invariance alone does not guarantee that the chain actually converges to $\pi$ from an arbitrary starting point. Two further properties are needed.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:irreducibility}
    A Markov chain with transition kernel $K$ is \emph{$\pi$-irreducible} if for every $\theta\in\Theta$ and every measurable set $A$ with $\pi(A)>0$ there exists an $n\ge 1$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        P(\theta_n\in A\mid\theta_0=\theta) > 0.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
In words, from any starting point, every region that carries positive target probability is reached with positive probability after finitely many steps. Without this property the chain could remain trapped in a part of $\Theta$ and never explore the rest of the support of $\pi$.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:aperiodicity}
    A $\pi$-irreducible Markov chain with transition kernel $K$ is said to be \emph{periodic} with period $d\ge 2$ if there exist disjoint measurable sets $E_0,\ldots,E_{d-1}\subseteq\Theta$, each with $\pi(E_i)>0$, such that
    \begin{equation}
        K(E_{(i+1)\bmod d}\mid\theta) = 1 \quad \text{for all } \theta\in E_i,\ i=0,\ldots,d-1.
    \end{equation}
    The chain is \emph{aperiodic} if it is not periodic for any $d\ge 2$.
\end{definition}
A periodic chain cycles through the sets $E_0,E_1,\ldots,E_{d-1},E_0,\ldots$, so the distribution of $\theta_m$ keeps oscillating and cannot converge, even though time averages may still converge.

\begin{theorem}[Ergodic theorem]
    \label{thm:ergodic_theorem}
    Let $\theta_0,\theta_1,\ldots$ be a $\pi$-irreducible Markov chain with invariant distribution $\pi$. Then $\pi$ is the unique invariant distribution, and the following hold for $\pi$-almost every starting point $\theta_0$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \emph{Law of large numbers.} For every function $g$ with $E_\pi[|g(\theta)|]<\infty$,
        \begin{equation}
            \hat{I}_M^{\mathrm{MCMC}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} g(\theta_m) \xrightarrow[M\to\infty]{a.s.} E_\pi[g(\theta)].
        \end{equation}
        \item \emph{Convergence in distribution.} If the chain is in addition aperiodic, the distribution of the $m$-th state converges to $\pi$,
        \begin{equation}
            \sup_A \left|P(\theta_m\in A\mid\theta_0) - \pi(A)\right| \xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{} 0.
        \end{equation}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The two statements concern different objects. The first is about the average over the whole trajectory $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_M$ as the number of iterations $M$ grows, and it does not require aperiodicity, since averaging over a full cycle removes any periodic behavior. The second is about the marginal distribution of a single state $\theta_m$ as its index $m$ grows, and it justifies treating late states of the chain as approximate draws from $\pi$. 

The MCMC estimator $\hat{I}_M^{\mathrm{MCMC}}$ plays the same role as the Monte Carlo estimator~\eqref{eq:monte_carlo_estimator}, with the important difference that the samples are now dependent and only asymptotically distributed according to $\pi$. In practice, the first iterations (the \emph{burn-in}) are therefore discarded.

\subsection{Metropolis-Hastings algorithm}
\label{sec:metropolis_hastings}
The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm \cite{metropolis1953equation,hastings1970monte} is a general recipe for constructing a transition kernel that satisfies detailed balance with respect to a given target $\pi$. It requires a \emph{proposal density} $q(\theta'\mid\theta)$, from which it is easy to sample, and which is not to be confused with the importance sampling proposals of the previous sections.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:mh_acceptance_probability}
    The \emph{Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability} of moving from $\theta$ to $\theta'$ is
    \begin{equation}
        \alpha(\theta,\theta') = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\pi(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\right\} = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\tilde{\pi}(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\tilde{\pi}(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\right\}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The normalizing constant $Z$ cancels in the ratio, so, exactly as for self-normalized importance sampling, only the unnormalized density $\tilde{\pi}$ is required. The resulting procedure is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Metropolis-Hastings}
\label{alg:metropolis_hastings}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Unnormalized target $\tilde{\pi}$, proposal density $q$, number of iterations $M$, initial state $\theta_0$ with $\tilde{\pi}(\theta_0)>0$.
    \FOR{$m=1,\ldots,M$}
        \STATE Sample $\theta' \sim q(\cdot\mid\theta_{m-1})$.
        \STATE Compute $\alpha(\theta_{m-1},\theta')$
        \STATE Sample $V \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$.
        \IF{$V \le \alpha(\theta_{m-1},\theta')$}
            \STATE Accept: set $\theta_m = \theta'$.
        \ELSE
            \STATE Reject: set $\theta_m = \theta_{m-1}$.
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

A proposed move is accepted with probability $\alpha(\theta,\theta')$; otherwise the chain stays where it is. The probability of staying at $\theta$ is therefore
\begin{equation}
    r(\theta) = 1 - \int \alpha(\theta,\theta')\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta',
\end{equation}
and the transition kernel of Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings} is
\begin{equation}
    K_{\mathrm{MH}}(\theta'\mid\theta) = \alpha(\theta,\theta')\,q(\theta'\mid\theta) + r(\theta)\,\delta(\theta'-\theta).
    \label{eq:mh_kernel}
\end{equation}
The first term corresponds to accepted proposals, and the second term, a point mass at the current state, to rejections.

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:mh_detailed_balance}
    The Metropolis-Hastings kernel~\eqref{eq:mh_kernel} satisfies detailed balance with respect to $\pi$. Consequently, $\pi$ is an invariant distribution of the Metropolis-Hastings chain.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Multiplying~\eqref{eq:mh_kernel} by $\pi(\theta)$ gives
    \begin{equation}
        \pi(\theta)\,K_{\mathrm{MH}}(\theta'\mid\theta) = \pi(\theta)\,\alpha(\theta,\theta')\,q(\theta'\mid\theta) + \pi(\theta)\,r(\theta)\,\delta(\theta'-\theta).
    \end{equation}
    We show that each of the two terms on the right-hand side is unchanged when $\theta$ and $\theta'$ are interchanged.

    For the first term, we have
    \begin{equation}
        \pi(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\min\left\{1,\, \frac{\pi(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\right\} = \min\left\{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta),\, \pi(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')\right\}.
    \end{equation}
    The right-hand side is symmetric in $\theta$ and $\theta'$, hence
    \begin{equation}
        \pi(\theta)\,\alpha(\theta,\theta')\,q(\theta'\mid\theta) = \pi(\theta')\,\alpha(\theta',\theta)\,q(\theta\mid\theta').
    \end{equation}

    Adding both terms gives $\pi(\theta)\,K_{\mathrm{MH}}(\theta'\mid\theta) = \pi(\theta')\,K_{\mathrm{MH}}(\theta\mid\theta')$. Invariance follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:detailed_balance_invariance}.
\end{proof}

The theorem holds for any proposal density $q$. However, invariance alone is not enough for Theorem~\ref{thm:ergodic_theorem}, which also requires irreducibility and aperiodicity, and these do depend on $q$. A simple sufficient condition is that $q(\theta'\mid\theta)>0$ for all $\theta,\theta'$ in the support of $\pi$. Then for every $\theta$ with $\pi(\theta)>0$ and every set $A$ with $\pi(A)>0$, the acceptance probability $\alpha(\theta,\theta')$ is positive for all $\theta'\in A$ with $\pi(\theta')>0$, so
\begin{equation}
    K_{\mathrm{MH}}(A\mid\theta) \ge \int_A \alpha(\theta,\theta')\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta' > 0.
\end{equation}
Every set of positive target probability is therefore reached in a single step, which gives $\pi$-irreducibility with $n=1$ in Definition~\ref{def:irreducibility}. It also rules out periodicity. If sets $E_0,\ldots,E_{d-1}$ as in Definition~\ref{def:aperiodicity} existed, then for $\theta\in E_0$ we would have $K_{\mathrm{MH}}(E_0\mid\theta)>0$, contradicting $K_{\mathrm{MH}}(E_1\mid\theta)=1$ since $E_0$ and $E_1$ are disjoint. Beyond these qualitative properties, the choice of $q$ determines how quickly the chain explores $\Theta$ and hence how many iterations are needed in practice. A common choice is the Gaussian random walk proposal $q(\theta'\mid\theta) = \mathcal{N}(\theta';\theta,\Sigma)$, which is symmetric, $q(\theta'\mid\theta) = q(\theta\mid\theta')$, so that the proposal densities cancel in~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_probability}.

Applied to Bayesian parameter inference with target~\eqref{eq:parameter_posterior}, we have $\tilde{\pi}(\theta) = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)$ and $Z = p(y_{1:T})$, so the acceptance probability becomes
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(\theta,\theta') = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta')\,p(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\right\}.
    \label{eq:mh_acceptance_parameters}
\end{equation}
The intractable evidence $p(y_{1:T})$ cancels, but the marginal likelihood $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ does not. For the MSSV model, and for nonlinear state-space models in general, Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings} therefore cannot be implemented directly. A natural idea is to replace $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ in~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_parameters} by a particle filter estimate. Remarkably, if this is done correctly, the resulting chain still targets the exact posterior. The key property that makes this possible is unbiasedness of the estimator, established in Theorem~\ref{thm:pf_marginal_likelihood_unbiased}.

\subsection{Pseudo-marginal Metropolis-Hastings}
\label{sec:pseudo_marginal_mh}
We now formalize what it means to replace the marginal likelihood in~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_parameters} by an estimate. Consider the following general setting \cite{andrieu2009pseudo}. For each $\theta$, let $U$ denote a collection of auxiliary random variables with distribution $\psi(u\mid\theta)$, and let $\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta,u)$ be an estimator of the marginal likelihood computed from $U=u$, satisfying
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta,u) \ge 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \int \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta,u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta).
    \label{eq:pseudo_marginal_unbiased}
\end{equation}

The \emph{pseudo-marginal Metropolis-Hastings} algorithm, given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh}, is Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings} with $p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ replaced by its estimate. It is crucial that the estimate associated with the current state is stored and reused in subsequent iterations, rather than recomputed. Recomputing it would yield a different algorithm that does not, in general, target the correct distribution.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Pseudo-marginal Metropolis-Hastings}
\label{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Prior $p(\theta)$, proposal density $q$, auxiliary distribution $\psi$, estimator $\hat{p}$, number of iterations $M$, initial state $\theta_0$.
    \STATE Sample $U_0 \sim \psi(\cdot\mid\theta_0)$ and compute $\hat{p}_0 = \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta_0,U_0)$; repeat until $p(\theta_0)\,\hat{p}_0 > 0$.
    \FOR{$m=1,\ldots,M$}
        \STATE Sample $\theta' \sim q(\cdot\mid\theta_{m-1})$.
        \STATE Sample $U' \sim \psi(\cdot\mid\theta')$ and compute $\hat{p}' = \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta',U')$.
        \STATE Compute
        \begin{equation*}
            \hat{\alpha} = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\hat{p}'\,p(\theta')\,q(\theta_{m-1}\mid\theta')}{\hat{p}_{m-1}\,p(\theta_{m-1})\,q(\theta'\mid\theta_{m-1})}\right\}.
        \end{equation*}
        \STATE Sample $V \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$.
        \IF{$V \le \hat{\alpha}$}
            \STATE Accept: set $(\theta_m,\hat{p}_m) = (\theta',\hat{p}')$.
        \ELSE
            \STATE Reject: set $(\theta_m,\hat{p}_m) = (\theta_{m-1},\hat{p}_{m-1})$.
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

At first sight, Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh} appears to be merely an approximation of Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings}. The key insight is that it is an \emph{exact} Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, but on an extended space that includes the auxiliary variables.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:extended_target}
    The \emph{extended target} on the space of $(\theta,u)$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{\pi}(\theta,u) = \frac{p(\theta)\,\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta,u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)}{p(y_{1:T})}.
        \label{eq:extended_target}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:pseudo_marginal_exact}
    Assume that the estimator satisfies~\eqref{eq:pseudo_marginal_unbiased}. Then:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\bar{\pi}$ is a probability density on the extended space;
        \item the marginal of $\bar{\pi}$ in $\theta$ is the posterior distribution, $\int \bar{\pi}(\theta,u)\,\mathrm{d}u = p(\theta\mid y_{1:T})$;
        \item the sequence $(\theta_m,U_m)_{m\ge 0}$ generated by Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh}, where $U_m$ denotes the auxiliary variables associated with the accepted estimate $\hat{p}_m$, is a Metropolis-Hastings chain with target $\bar{\pi}$ and proposal
        \begin{equation}
            Q(\theta',u'\mid\theta,u) = q(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\psi(u'\mid\theta').
        \end{equation}
    \end{enumerate}
    Consequently, $\bar{\pi}$ is invariant for $(\theta_m,U_m)_{m\ge 0}$, and if $(\theta_{m-1},U_{m-1}) \sim \bar{\pi}$, then $\theta_m \sim p(\theta\mid y_{1:T})$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    \emph{1.} Clearly $\bar{\pi}\ge 0$. Integrating first over $u$ and using~\eqref{eq:pseudo_marginal_unbiased},
    \begin{equation}
        \iint \bar{\pi}(\theta,u)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \frac{1}{p(y_{1:T})}\int p(\theta) \left(\int \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta,u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\mathrm{d}\theta = \frac{\int p(\theta)\,p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta}{p(y_{1:T})} = 1.
    \end{equation}

    \emph{2.} Again by~\eqref{eq:pseudo_marginal_unbiased} and by~\eqref{eq:parameter_posterior},
    \begin{equation}
        \int \bar{\pi}(\theta,u)\,\mathrm{d}u = \frac{p(\theta)\,p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}{p(y_{1:T})} = p(\theta\mid y_{1:T}).
    \end{equation}

    \emph{3.} Consider Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings} on the extended space with target $\bar{\pi}$ and proposal $Q$. Proposing from $Q$ amounts to sampling $\theta'\sim q(\cdot\mid\theta)$ and then $u'\sim\psi(\cdot\mid\theta')$, which is precisely what Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh} does. The Metropolis-Hastings ratio on the extended space is
    \begin{align}
        \frac{\bar{\pi}(\theta',u')\,Q(\theta,u\mid\theta',u')}{\bar{\pi}(\theta,u)\,Q(\theta',u'\mid\theta,u)}
        &= \frac{p(\theta')\,\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta',u')\,\psi(u'\mid\theta')\;q(\theta\mid\theta')\,\psi(u\mid\theta)}{p(\theta)\,\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta,u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\;q(\theta'\mid\theta)\,\psi(u'\mid\theta')} \\
        &= \frac{\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta',u')\,p(\theta')\,q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta,u)\,p(\theta)\,q(\theta'\mid\theta)},
    \end{align}
    since the evidence $p(y_{1:T})$ and the densities of the auxiliary variables $\psi$ cancel. This is exactly the ratio in $\hat{\alpha}$ of Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh}, where $\hat{p}_{m-1} = \hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta_{m-1},U_{m-1})$. Moreover, on acceptance both $\theta$ and $u$ are replaced, and on rejection both are kept. Since the algorithm uses $u$ only through the value $\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta,u)$, storing $\hat{p}_m$ is equivalent to storing $U_m$. Hence Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh} is Algorithm~\ref{alg:metropolis_hastings} on the extended space.

    By Theorem~\ref{thm:mh_detailed_balance}, the chain $(\theta_m,U_m)_{m\ge 0}$ satisfies detailed balance with respect to $\bar{\pi}$, so $\bar{\pi}$ is invariant. If $(\theta_{m-1},U_{m-1})\sim\bar{\pi}$, then $(\theta_m,U_m)\sim\bar{\pi}$, and by the second claim the marginal distribution of $\theta_m$ is $p(\theta\mid y_{1:T})$.
\end{proof}

The proof also explains why the estimate of the current state must not be recomputed. Reusing $\hat{p}_{m-1}$ corresponds to keeping $U_{m-1}$ as part of the state of the chain, whereas drawing a fresh $U$ for the current state at every iteration would alter the transition kernel, and the resulting kernel no longer satisfies detailed balance with respect to $\bar{\pi}$. The proof also shows that unbiasedness is exactly the property needed. Without it, the $\theta$-marginal of $\bar{\pi}$ would be proportional to $p(\theta)\,E_\psi[\hat{p}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta,U)]$ instead of to the posterior.

\subsection{Particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings}
\label{sec:pmmh}
Particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings (PMMH) \cite{andrieu2010particle} is the pseudo-marginal Metropolis-Hastings algorithm in which the estimator is the particle filter marginal likelihood estimator~\eqref{eq:pf_marginal_likelihood_estimator}. Here the auxiliary variables $U$ consist of all the random variables generated by one run of the bootstrap particle filter at parameter $\theta$, that is, the initial particles, the propagated particles at every time step and the outcomes of all resampling steps. Their joint distribution $\psi(u\mid\theta)$ is implicitly defined by Algorithm~\ref{alg:bootstrap_particle_filter} and never needs to be evaluated, since it cancels in the acceptance ratio. The complete procedure is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:pmmh}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings}
\label{alg:pmmh}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Observations $y_{1:T}$, number of particles $N$, prior $p(\theta)$, proposal density $q$, number of iterations $M$, initial state $\theta_0$.
    \STATE Run Algorithm~\ref{alg:bootstrap_particle_filter} at $\theta_0$ and compute $\hat{p}_0 = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta_0)$.
    \FOR{$m=1,\ldots,M$}
        \STATE Sample $\theta' \sim q(\cdot\mid\theta_{m-1})$.
        \STATE Run Algorithm~\ref{alg:bootstrap_particle_filter} at $\theta'$ and compute $\hat{p}' = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta')$.
        \STATE Compute
        \begin{equation*}
            \hat{\alpha} = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\hat{p}'\,p(\theta')\,q(\theta_{m-1}\mid\theta')}{\hat{p}_{m-1}\,p(\theta_{m-1})\,q(\theta'\mid\theta_{m-1})}\right\}.
        \end{equation*}
        \STATE Sample $V \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$.
        \IF{$V \le \hat{\alpha}$}
            \STATE Accept: set $(\theta_m,\hat{p}_m) = (\theta',\hat{p}')$.
        \ELSE
            \STATE Reject: set $(\theta_m,\hat{p}_m) = (\theta_{m-1},\hat{p}_{m-1})$.
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:pmmh_exact}
    For every number of particles $N\ge 1$, the particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings chain admits an invariant distribution whose marginal in $\theta$ is the exact posterior distribution $p(\theta\mid y_{1:T})$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    The estimator $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is a product of averages of densities and is therefore non-negative. By Theorem~\ref{thm:pf_marginal_likelihood_unbiased}, it is unbiased for every $N\ge 1$ and every $\theta$, that is,
    \begin{equation}
        \int \hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta,u)\,\psi(u\mid\theta)\,\mathrm{d}u = p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta),
    \end{equation}
    where the dependence on the particle filter randomness $u$ has been made explicit. Condition~\eqref{eq:pseudo_marginal_unbiased} is thus satisfied, and Algorithm~\ref{alg:pmmh} is a special case of Algorithm~\ref{alg:pseudo_marginal_mh}. The claim follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:pseudo_marginal_exact}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    The auxiliary variables of the particle filter include discrete resampling indices, so $\psi(u\mid\theta)$ is in general not a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure. As with the Dirac delta notation used throughout this chapter, the integrals over $u$ should be understood with respect to the appropriate probability measure. The proofs of Theorems~\ref{thm:mh_detailed_balance} and~\ref{thm:pseudo_marginal_exact} carry over without change, since $\psi$ only ever appears as the distribution from which $u'$ is proposed and cancels in the acceptance ratio.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:pmmh_exact} shows that PMMH is not an approximation: the number of particles does not affect the invariant distribution, only how efficiently the chain explores it. Under mild additional conditions, essentially that the corresponding exact Metropolis-Hastings chain would be irreducible and aperiodic, the PMMH chain also converges to its invariant distribution \cite{andrieu2010particle}. The role of $N$ is best understood by noting that the acceptance ratio of PMMH equals the ideal ratio~\eqref{eq:mh_acceptance_parameters} multiplied by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta')/p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta')}{\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)/p(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)}.
\end{equation}
If the estimate at the current state happens to be much larger than the true likelihood, this factor is small for most proposals, and the chain may get stuck at the same value for many iterations. A small $N$ produces noisy estimates and hence poor mixing, while a large $N$ makes every iteration expensive. As a rule of thumb, \cite{doucet2015efficient} recommend that $N$ should be chosen such that the standard deviation of $\log\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ around the posterior mode is approximately between $1$ and $1.7$.

In practice, the acceptance ratio is computed on the logarithmic scale to avoid numerical underflow, since $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{PF}}(y_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ is a product of $T$ factors. Moreover, if the proposed $\theta'$ lies outside the support of the prior, then $p(\theta')=0$ and the proposal can be rejected immediately, without running the particle filter.

\begin{remark}
    Algorithm~\ref{alg:pmmh} targets only the posterior distribution of the parameters. It is shown in \cite{andrieu2010particle} that PMMH can be extended to target the joint posterior $p(\theta,x_{0:T}\mid y_{1:T})$ as well. Each time the particle filter is run at a proposed $\theta'$, a trajectory $X_{0:T}'$ is additionally drawn from its empirical smoothing distribution, and this trajectory is accepted or rejected together with $\theta'$. The acceptance probability is unchanged.
\end{remark}
